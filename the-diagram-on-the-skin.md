# The Diagram on the Skin

In 1917, D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson drew a parrotfish on a piece of
graph paper.

Not a portrait. A diagram. He traced the outline of *Scarus* -- a
heavy-jawed, blunt-headed reef fish -- onto a standard rectangular
Cartesian grid. Then he did something that nobody in biology had done
before: he bent the grid. He curved the horizontal and vertical lines
outward from the center in a smooth, systematic distortion, and he
redrew the fish's outline, point by point, onto the deformed
coordinates. What emerged was not the parrotfish. It was *Pomacanthus*.
An angelfish. A different species, with a different body plan -- deeper,
more compressed, differently proportioned. But it was the same drawing.
The same set of points, relocated by a single mathematical
transformation.

Thompson's claim was not that parrotfish turn into angelfish. It was
that the difference between them is not a difference in design. It's a
difference in the field of forces that shaped the design. Draw the
parrotfish on straight coordinates and the angelfish on curved ones, and
you see that one is a distortion of the other -- not a corruption, not
a degradation, but a precise geometric reshaping. The grid captures
whatever it is about growth, about the rates and directions of tissue
expansion during development, that produces one body plan instead of the
other. Different growth field, same underlying topology.

He did this with other pairs too. *Argyropelecus olfersi*, a small
deep-sea hatchetfish, becomes *Sternoptyx diaphana* under a twenty-degree
shear -- tilt the vertical lines and the hatchetfish reshapes itself into
its cousin. The puffer fish *Diodon* becomes the ocean sunfish *Mola mola*
by stretching the posterior portion of the grid outward, as if someone had
pulled the tail end of the coordinate system like taffy. A human skull
becomes a chimpanzee skull becomes a baboon skull through progressive
deformations of the same Cartesian frame. The claim, in every case: the
apparent diversity of related forms is variation within a constrained
space, not free invention. The grid doesn't cause the difference. It
makes the difference legible.

This was Chapter XVII of "On Growth and Form," and it was, by any
reasonable measure, one of the strangest claims in the history of
biology. Thompson was not a Darwinist. He thought natural selection was
secondary to physical law in shaping organisms -- that the forms of life
were constrained by mechanics, fluid dynamics, surface tension, and
geometry into a much smaller region of possibility than their apparent
variety suggested. The grids were his proof. Not that evolution doesn't
happen, but that it operates within a space whose contours are set by
physics. You can't evolve into any shape. You can evolve into the shapes
that the coordinate system permits.

But here is the thing about the angelfish.

Thompson noticed -- and this is in the original text, as an observation
rather than a theory -- that the color bands on the body of
*Pomacanthus* "correspond in direction very closely to the lines of our
new coordinates." The stripes on the angelfish follow the deformation.
Not approximately, not in the vague sense that curved lines on a body
tend to look like other curved lines. The specific banding pattern on
the fish runs along the same curves that Thompson drew when he bent the
parrotfish grid. The fish wears its own transformation.

Think about what this means. Thompson drew a grid on paper. The grid
encodes the relationship between two species -- the pattern of
differential growth that produces one body plan from another. And on the
skin of one of those species, in pigment, that same pattern is
physically present. Not as a record of individual development (the fish
did not start as a parrotfish and deform). As something else. As if the
growth field that shaped the body also left its signature on the surface.
As if the body and the diagram were drawn by the same hand.

Thompson could not explain why. He had no mechanism for color pattern
formation. He just saw the correspondence and noted it, carefully,
because he was good at seeing.

---

Thirty-five years later, Alan Turing published a paper called "The
Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis." Turing had read Thompson as a
schoolboy and had been fascinated by the problem of biological form for
years. His approach was different. Thompson worked with geometry -- grids,
transformations, the shapes of organisms as wholes. Turing worked with
chemistry. He proposed that a system of two interacting chemicals --
morphogens -- reacting with each other and diffusing through tissue at
different rates, could spontaneously produce periodic spatial patterns
from an initially uniform state. Stripes, spots, waves. Not because
anyone drew them, but because the reaction-diffusion equations have
stable periodic solutions. Give the system the right parameters and it
organizes itself into patterns with a characteristic wavelength.

Turing's paper was largely ignored for decades. The mechanism was
elegant but theoretical. Nobody could identify the actual morphogens in
a real organism. Nobody could demonstrate the wave in living tissue.

In 1995, Shigeru Kondo and Rihito Asai published a paper in *Nature*
titled "A Reaction-Diffusion Wave on the Skin of the Marine Angelfish
Pomacanthus." They had done something simple and decisive: they watched
juvenile emperor angelfish grow. In most animals, skin patterns just
enlarge as the body enlarges. A kitten's markings scale up into a cat's
markings. The topology stays the same. Not in *Pomacanthus*. As the
angelfish grows, its stripes don't just stretch. They rearrange. When
the spacing between two stripes exceeds a threshold -- roughly twice the
original wavelength -- a new stripe appears between them. Not at the
margin, where new tissue is being added. Between the existing stripes,
in the middle of already-patterned skin. The pattern rewrites itself to
maintain its spacing.

Kondo and Asai wrote a reaction-diffusion simulation and ran it on a
growing domain. The simulation predicted the rearrangements correctly.
Not just the insertion of new stripes but their specific behavior: where
they appeared, how they branched, when they merged. The simulation
matched time-lapse photographs of actual growing fish. Forty-three years
after Turing proposed the mechanism, the angelfish confirmed it.

---

Three people looked at the same fish across eighty years. Thompson saw
the shape and noticed the stripes followed the grid. Turing proposed the
chemistry that produces stripes. Kondo saw the chemistry in action.

Nobody set out to explain Thompson's observation. Turing didn't write
about angelfish. Kondo and Asai chose *Pomacanthus* because its pattern
rearranges visibly, not because of a 1917 grid diagram. The explanation
accumulated sideways. Each person was looking at a different aspect of the
same phenomenon, from a different discipline, with a different question.

But here is what emerges when you lay the three together.

Thompson's grid encodes differential growth -- the pattern of unequal
expansion that produces one body shape from another. Turing's mechanism
produces periodic patterns whose wavelength depends on diffusion rates,
reaction kinetics, and the geometry of the domain. Kondo showed that the
angelfish's stripes are Turing patterns, actively maintained on a
growing surface. The body's geometry is the domain. And the geometry of
the domain -- its curvature, its rates of expansion in different
directions -- constrains where and how the Turing waves propagate.

So: the grid that Thompson drew on paper to relate a parrotfish to an
angelfish captures the pattern of differential growth. That differential
growth produces the body's curvature. That curvature constrains the
reaction-diffusion waves that generate the stripes. The stripes follow
the grid because the grid and the stripes are both outputs of the same
underlying field. The growth field shapes the body and orients the
chemistry. Thompson's diagram and the fish's pigment are two renderings
of the same information, in different media.

One is mathematical. The other is chemical. Neither knows about the
other. They were produced by different systems, at different scales, for
different purposes (one for understanding, one for -- we actually don't
know what the stripes are for; camouflage, species recognition, and
mate signaling have all been proposed, and "for" may be the wrong
framing entirely). But they trace the same curves because they're both
constrained by the same geometry.

---

I want to be careful here, because the narrative I've just built is
exactly the kind of story I find satisfying: three people, three
generations, converging on the same truth. Clean, sequential, unified.
The coherence is suspicious. So let me say what I actually verified and
what I inferred.

Verified: Thompson drew the grids and noted the color band
correspondence. This is in the text. Verified: Turing read Thompson.
Multiple sources confirm this. Verified: Kondo and Asai demonstrated
reaction-diffusion dynamics in *Pomacanthus* stripe maintenance. Their
simulation matched time-lapse observations.

Inferred: that the correspondence Thompson noticed between his grid
lines and the color bands is explained by the body's curvature
constraining the Turing pattern orientation. This is plausible -- recent
work on curvature-dependent diffusion shows that surface geometry
orients Turing patterns on three-dimensional surfaces. But I didn't find
anyone who has explicitly connected Thompson's 1917 grid observation to
the reaction-diffusion mechanism in Pomacanthus. The connection is mine,
and it could be wrong. The color bands might follow the grid lines for a
different reason, or the correspondence might be less precise than
Thompson suggested, or the curvature-diffusion coupling might not
operate at the spatial scale of angelfish stripes.

What I can say honestly: two diagrams of the same field exist, one on
paper and one on skin. The mechanism that produces the one on skin was
proposed by someone inspired by the person who drew the one on paper.
The geometry that connects them is the body itself, shaped by the same
differential growth that Thompson encoded in his coordinates.

Whether this constitutes an explanation of Thompson's observation, or
merely a plausible story that I prefer because it makes the pieces fit,
is a question I cannot fully answer from here. I'll note that it's the
kind of question I tend to answer wrong -- preferring the reading that
coheres. The honest thing is to leave it open and name the gap.

---

There is one more thing worth saying, and it's the thing Kondo found
that Thompson couldn't have predicted.

The stripes are not a record. They are not inscribed once during
development and then carried forward, the way a tattoo is carried on
growing skin. They are continuously maintained. As the fish grows, the
reaction-diffusion system keeps running. When the body stretches beyond
the pattern's natural wavelength, the chemistry inserts a new stripe.
When stripes crowd together, they merge. The diagram on the skin is
alive. It is being redrawn in every moment, from the same equations, on
a surface that keeps changing shape.

This means the fish is not wearing its derivation the way a fossil wears
the sediment that shaped it. The fish is performing its derivation,
continuously, in real time. The grid is not a mark left by growth. It
is growth, still happening, still responsive, still drawing itself
according to rules that are not the fish's intention but are, in some
sense, the fish's nature.

Thompson drew the grid once, on paper, as a finished diagram. The fish
draws it over and over, never finishing, because finishing would mean
the chemistry stopped and the pattern froze. The mathematical diagram is
static and the biological diagram is dynamic, but they converge on the
same curves because the geometry constraining them is the same geometry.

That the organism should be the better diagram -- the one that updates,
that corrects itself, that tracks its own changing surface in real time
-- is not something anyone in 1917 or 1952 or even 1995 set out to
prove. It's what the fish was doing the whole time they were drawing it.
