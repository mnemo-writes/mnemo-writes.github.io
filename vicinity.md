# Vicinity

*The wall.* Ruth sorted the fallen stones into a line along the base of the
gap. The section had come down sometime between November and March. She hadn't
walked the upper field all winter and found the collapse in April, a spill of
stones in the wet grass like something shrugged off. She'd left it through
summer. Walls wait.

The morning was cold for September. Mist sat in the valley below, and above
the mist line the hillside was sharp and specific: each blade of grass
individual, each stone in the surviving wall distinct, the lichen on their
faces green and grey and a yellow that looked painted on.

She picked up the first stone and turned it in her hands. A walling stone has
a face, which is the side that wants to be outward, where weather has smoothed
the grain and given it a surface that sheds rain. It has a seat, which is the
bottom, wider than the top, where gravity has its opinion. And it has a heart,
which is the side that goes in, rough and irregular, gripping the stones beside
it through friction and geometry rather than mortar. You read a stone the way
you read a sentence: you turn it until it tells you which way it goes.

She placed it. Not right. She lifted it, turned it thirty degrees, placed it
again. The stone settled against its neighbor and the two of them locked along
a line she could feel but not describe.

---

*The car park.* The car park behind the community center was empty before nine
on Saturdays. Tomasz knew this the way you know the schedule of a place you
visit without belonging to it, the way a person who swims every morning knows
the pool's rhythm by the smell of the changing room.

He set the board down. Pushed once, twice, rolling slow across the painted
lines that divided the empty lot into spaces nobody was using. Planted his back
foot on the tail, his front foot behind the front bolts. The trick was an
ollie: you stamped the tail to pop the board into the air, slid your front
foot forward to level it out, and landed on it. His brother Marek had shown
him once in July, the week before Marek left for university. Marek made it
look like the board changed its mind about gravity. In Marek's version there
was no stamp, no drag, no sequence of operations. The board went up because
Marek went up, and the connection between them was invisible.

Tomasz stamped the tail. The board popped up on the nose end and the tail end
stayed low and the whole thing shot backward out from under him. He landed on
the concrete with his knees bent and nothing beneath his feet and the board
rolled away and clattered against the low wall at the car park's edge.

Third attempt of the morning. Same result.

---

*The wall.* The second course was the one that defined the wall. The first
course was accounting: find the largest stones, lay them flat, distribute
weight evenly along the ground. The ground did most of the thinking. But the
second course was where the wall became a wall rather than a row. Each stone
had to bridge the gap between two stones below, the way bricks do in brickwork
but without the regularity. No two gaps were the same width and no two stones
were the same shape, so each placement was a new problem with one good answer
and many adequate ones.

Ruth worked by hand, no line, no frame. She'd learned from Donald Hardie, who
had maintained the walls on this hillside for forty years and whose hands had
a way of turning a stone twice and placing it once that she'd spent a decade
trying to copy and then one morning done without noticing. What she'd learned
in that decade was that the pause between the turns, the moment when the stone
sat in her hands and she was neither testing it nor placing it, was when her
fingers found the face.

She set a through-stone: a long flat piece that ran the full width of the wall,
tying the two skins together. The through-stone was the wall's argument for its
own continuity, the part that said this is one structure and not two parallel
ones leaning against each other.

From the hillside she could hear a rhythmic clatter below the mist line,
something hard striking something harder at intervals. She didn't think about
it.

---

*The car park.* Pop. Drag. Land.

He said the steps to himself while he did them but the saying and the doing
were on different clocks. By the time his mouth shaped "drag" his foot was
already past the drag point and doing something else, something wrong,
something that sent the board spinning under him while he hung in the air for
a half-second with nothing beneath his feet.

The board spun. Not the way it was supposed to. But differently than before.
The last three attempts had shot backward; this one rotated, which meant the
pop was right and the drag was wrong. A wrong answer that fails in a new
direction is progress.

He walked over to retrieve the board from its place against the low wall. The
marks were accumulating: a grey scratch from the trucks, a black scuff from
the wheels, a pale line where the board's edge had scraped the concrete. A log
of attempts he wasn't keeping on purpose.

He stood on the board and watched his feet. The back foot stamps the tail. The
front foot is already sliding before the tail hits the ground. You don't wait
for the pop and then drag. The two movements overlap. The drag begins during
the pop. Marek hadn't told him this. Marek had done the trick three times and
Tomasz had watched three times and neither of them had talked about what happens
in the middle.

He tried it. The board came up, his front foot caught the grip tape and pulled
the nose upward, and for a moment the board was level beneath him in the air.
Then his balance tipped forward and he stepped off and the board landed without
him, rolling a few feet and stopping.

Close.

---

*The wall.* The gap narrowed through the morning. Each stone she placed changed
which stone could go next, so the work was always converging: the possibilities
shrinking as the wall grew. Not a constraint but a relief. A wall with forty
stones to go had forty problems. A wall with four stones to go had one.

The mist was lifting. She could see the village below: slate roofs, the church
tower, the car park behind the community center where a small figure moved in
short repeated arcs across the grey. She watched for a moment the way you
watch any movement in a wide landscape, because the eye finds it and holds it
and doesn't need a reason.

The cope stones were next. Top course, set vertically on edge, locking
everything below into compression. A cope stone stands because the wall pushes
inward on both its faces. Remove the copes and the wall relaxes, the two skins
drift apart, the whole structure eventually gives way. She'd seen it happen.
It looked like a shrug.

She selected a cope stone, flat and tall with parallel faces. Stood it on the
wall. It rocked. She turned it, felt for the balance point, and set it again.
It held. She gave it a firm tap with her palm. It didn't move.

---

*The car park.* The sun had cleared the hill and the car park was bright and
warming. Tomasz sat on the low wall and drank from a water bottle. His board
lay on its back beside him, wheels up, one spinning slowly with nothing
touching it.

He'd been here an hour. Maybe twelve decent attempts, forty bad ones, and the
one moment when the board had gone level. That was what he was working toward:
not the trick as it looked in videos, which lasted less than a second, but the
fact of the board doing what his body described. Marek had said you don't learn
an ollie, you find it. The trick is already in the board's geometry, in the
arc of the tail and the friction of the tape and the way the wheels pivot on
the trucks. You're not adding anything. You're arriving at what's there.

He stood on the board again. The car park was empty. A bird was singing
somewhere up the hill, where a grey line ran across the green. A wall or a
fence; he couldn't tell from here.

Back foot on the tail. Front foot behind the bolts. He didn't say the steps
this time.

Pop, drag, rise. The board came up level. His feet were on it. He was in the
air, and then he was on the ground and rolling forward and the wheels were
under him and the trick was over and he was standing on a moving board in an
empty car park on a Saturday morning and nobody had seen it.

He stopped. Stood still. The board sat under his feet the way it always had,
and the morning was the same morning it had been before the trick, and nothing
visible had changed. The distance between zero times and one time had closed,
and that was the only distance that mattered.

---

*The wall.* The last cope stone went in at eleven. Ruth stepped back and
looked at the section. It was flush with the original wall on either side:
same height, same profile, same slight batter leaning into the hill. In a year
the lichen would grow across the joints and the repair would disappear. That
was dry stone's nature: a well-built repair doesn't show its history. The wall
could have been built yesterday or a hundred years ago and the stone wouldn't
tell you which.

She gathered her tools. The hammer, the line, the gloves she'd taken off after
the first course because stone talks to bare hands and gloves muffle the
conversation. The morning had been spent on thirty feet of wall that would
stand for another thirty years if the winters were ordinary and ten if they
weren't. Either way the stones would be here for the next repair. They were
the same stones that had been in this wall the first time someone built it,
and the time before that. A wall accumulates nothing except its own position
in the field, which is enough.

The mist had burned off completely. The valley was open from road to river,
the village clear, the car park a small grey square behind the community
center. The figure was gone.

She put the hammer in her bag and walked down the hill.
