# Weigh-off

The forklift was Gary's, which meant Gary drove it, which meant everyone stood
well back.

"It's on the pallet," said Bev, who ran the scale and the clipboard and, since
2019, the whole regional weigh-off, because nobody else had wanted to after the
thing with the tarp. "Gary. It's on. You can stop lifting."

"I'm settling it."

"It's a pumpkin. It's settled. It's been settled since August."

The pumpkin belonged to Ron Pelkey and was named Deborah, after nobody. Ron
said the name came to him. It was the colour of a traffic cone that had been
left out, and it had the shape giant pumpkins get when they stop being round
and start being about gravity: a flat bottom, a slumped top, shoulders like a
sofa. Ron had driven it four hours in a horse trailer lined with egg-crate
foam and had not, according to his wife, spoken the whole way.

"Before we lift," Bev said, "inspection."

"It's fine," Ron said.

"I know it's fine. I have to look anyway. Kev, the light."

Kevin was fourteen and Bev's nephew and held the flashlight like it might go
off. Bev got down on her knees on the cardboard and went around the blossom
end with her face very close to the skin, the way you'd read small print.

"You've had a split here."

"That's healed."

"I can see it's healed. I'm writing it down."

"It doesn't go into the cavity."

"I didn't say it did. I said I'm writing it down." She wrote it down. "If it
went into the cavity we wouldn't be having a nice conversation, we'd be having
the other conversation."

"What's the other conversation," said Kevin.

"Where I say disqualified and Ron says a lot of words in front of you."

"I wouldn't," Ron said.

"You did in 2021."

"That was a soft spot. That was different. That was the stem end and you
pushed on it."

"I pressed on it. With one finger. And it went in."

"It went in because you pressed it."

"It went in because it was rotten, Ron."

There were about forty people in the parking lot of the Agway, most of them
growers, standing in the particular way growers stand at weigh-offs, which is
with their arms folded so nobody can see them hoping. Linda Tremblay's
pumpkin, which was next, was still on her trailer under a quilt. Linda had
not taken the quilt off. Linda would not take the quilt off until Deborah was
weighed, and everyone knew why, and nobody said it.

"Okay," Bev said. "Gary. Sling."

Gary got down off the forklift. The sling was a ring of nylon webbing that
went under the pumpkin's shoulders and up to the forks, and it took Gary,
Ron, Ron's son-in-law and a man nobody knew to get it seated. The man nobody
knew turned out to be from the newspaper and had only meant to take a photo.

"Don't let it ride up," Ron said.

"It's not riding up."

"It's riding up on that side."

"That's the shape of it, Ron. Your pumpkin's lopsided."

"It's not lopsided, it's—"

"It's lopsided," said four people.

Gary got back on the forklift. He lifted. The pallet came free of the ground
by about an inch and hung there, and the sling creaked, and Deborah swung very
slightly to the left, toward the lopsided side, and stopped.

Everyone looked at the readout. Bev looked at the readout. Kevin, who had been
told to hold the light on the pumpkin, held it on the readout, and Bev took it
off him and pointed it back at the pumpkin.

"We're waiting for it to stop moving."

"It's stopped."

"The number hasn't stopped."

The number was going up and down by two pounds, which was the wind, or the
forklift's engine, or Ron breathing. Then it went down by two and stayed.

Bev wrote it on the clipboard first. She always wrote it first and said it
second, ever since a man in 2017 had argued she'd said a different number
from the one she wrote.

"Pallet tare is thirty-eight," she said. "Sling is on the forks so it's not
in. Gross is eighteen hundred and forty-four. Net is eighteen hundred and
six."

Ron did the arithmetic in his head, though she had just done it, and then
did it again.

"Is that good," said Kevin.

"It's very good," Bev said. "Put it down, Gary. Slow."

Gary put it down slow. Ron's son-in-law shook Ron's hand. The man from the
newspaper took the photo after all. Ron put his palm flat on the top of the
pumpkin, on the slumped bit, and kept it there for a while, and then he took
it off and wiped it on his jeans, because there was dew.

On her trailer, Linda Tremblay started folding back the quilt.

"Next," said Bev. "Linda. Gary, back it up. Kev, go get the other sling, this
one's wet."
