She'd been working on the arm for three hours when the chromatophore expanded.

Not the arm in the prep dish. The scrap she'd set aside on a damp paper towel -- a two-centimeter disk of skin cut from the posterior mantle before she started teasing connectives. It sat under the bench lamp, disconnected from every nerve that could have told it to do anything.

Mei almost missed it. A dark bloom in her peripheral vision, there and gone in two seconds. She looked. The skin sat still, stippled with its resting pattern. Then she noticed the bench lamp angled across it, its warm cone ending at the paper towel's edge.

She moved the lamp.

One chromatophore expanded. Then three. Then a cluster of five, each stretching from pinpoint to dark disk as the light crossed them, contracting as it passed.

She moved the lamp again, slowly, left to right. The expansion tracked the light across the tissue. Sluggish compared to a living animal -- seconds rather than fractions -- but consistent. She pulled the lamp away. The chromatophores contracted to their resting state.

She sat on her stool and held still.

Her dissertation was about motor control. Specifically, how the central nervous system coordinates millions of chromatophores into the coherent patterns of cephalopod camouflage. She had preliminary data showing stellate ganglion modulation of pattern frequency in the posterior mantle. Her electrode array, half-assembled on the adjacent bench, was designed to record what the brain told the skin to do.

This skin had no brain. The ganglion was in a separate dish. The central nervous system was in the freezer.

She turned off the bench lamp and switched on the fiber optic illuminator, its narrow beam peaked at 480 nanometers. Cold blue-white. She directed the point at the skin through the dissection scope.

The chromatophores responded faster to the blue than they had to the lamp.

480 was rhodopsin's peak. The same photopigment the retina used, at the same spectral sensitivity. She knew about dermal photoreception as a phenomenon -- a paper she'd read in journal club two years ago and filed under tangential. Tangential meant it didn't connect to the question she was asking. The question she was asking was how the brain produced coordinated pattern.

What she was looking at was pattern without a brain.

---

She ran controls for a week before telling anyone. Fresh tissue, different wavelengths, dark-adapted samples, pharmacological blocks on the phototransduction cascade. The response was real, reproducible, and strongest in blue. She wasn't chasing an artifact.

Part of why she waited was that the finding felt too clean. Part was that she couldn't yet say how it changed her project, only that it did.

When her parents asked what her dissertation was about, she'd always given the same answer. The octopus brain controls millions of tiny color cells in its skin. She'd built a year of work on that sentence. But the sentence was wrong in the way that requires a different sentence, not a correction.

The brain doesn't control the chromatophores entirely. Not locally. The chromatophores carry light-detecting proteins in the epithelial surface, each cell responsive to the light falling on its particular patch of body, without routing through a central eye or a central anything.

Not an eye. The geometry was wrong for an eye -- no lens, no focal plane, no image formation. Each cell answered only for its own position. The skin didn't see the world. Each point on the skin felt the light at that point, and matched it.

Mei picked up the tungsten needle she'd been using to trace motor nerves. She looked at her electrode array, its sixty-four gold contacts arranged to record central commands traveling outward. The whole apparatus assumed a direction. Brain to skin. Signal to display. Instruction to response.

She put the needle down and looked at the skin under the fiber optic. The chromatophores nearest the light had expanded, darkening the surface directly under the beam. The cells farther away sat at their resting state. No coordination. No pattern. Just each cell, where it was, responding to what touched it.

She had been asking what the brain tells the skin. The skin had its own answer, and it had been giving it the whole time, in a signal she'd run her electrode leads straight past.

She turned the fiber optic off. The chromatophores settled back into the resting stipple.

She turned it on.

They expanded.
