# Diagnosis

She took the long way home from Sara's. Twenty minutes on Maple
instead of twelve through the school lot. Something about the
evening needed the extra distance, not to process -- Claire didn't
believe in walking as processing, which seemed to her like shaking
a snowglobe and calling the settling a conclusion -- but to hold
the shape of it a little longer before she set it down.

Sara had been off.

Claire kept arriving at the word and rejecting it. "Off" implied
a normal, and Sara didn't have one the way most people did. She was
seasonal, given to stretches of intensity followed by quiet weeks
where she didn't return texts and then returned them all at once
with "sorry, was in a hole." Those holes didn't worry Claire. They
were Sara being Sara, the way a porch light that flickers on windy
nights isn't broken, just loosely wired.

Tonight wasn't the hole. In the hole, Sara went flat -- low energy,
wide topics, steady eating. Tonight she'd been present but angled,
like a picture hung from one nail. Attentive and slightly wrong.

She'd asked about Claire's job, for one thing.

They'd agreed years ago that Claire's job was boring in the way
that made it impossible to explain why it was boring, and Sara had
stopped asking, which was one of the better things about her.

"How's the library?" she'd said, after the wine came.

"Same building, same books."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the same answer I always give."

"Right," Sara had said, and let it go in a way that wasn't letting
it go.

Then, over bread, looking at the candle: "Do you ever feel like
you're maintaining something?"

Claire had taken the question as being about David. It had the
shape of a marriage question -- the kind you asked when you meant
I am taking care of something that doesn't need me, or that needs
me only the way a house needs someone to pay the gas bill.

"Maintaining what?"

"I don't know. A frequency. A version of something."

"Of what?"

Sara had laughed and eaten the bread. "Never mind. I'm being
weird."

She was being weird, and Claire let it drop the way you let
someone's weirdness drop when you're saving it to think about
later.

She was thinking about it now, on Maple, under the oaks that had
started to turn but hadn't committed.

David was fine. Claire had always thought David was fine. He was a
fine man with a fine job and a fine temperament, and the trouble
with fine was that it didn't tell you what was underneath. Fine was
a thermostat setting, not a temperature. Sara had married him at
thirty-one after two years of dating that Sara described, without
obvious irony, as "comfortable." Comfortable was even less
diagnostic. It could mean harmony, or it could mean nothing left
to rub against.

They hadn't done this in a while. Four months, maybe five.
Claire had canceled the spring dinner for a work thing, genuinely
unavoidable, and rescheduled March because her sister was visiting.
The sister part was true. The whole reason was harder to name: she
hadn't felt like it. Not hadn't felt like Sara. Just hadn't felt
like the effort of an evening, the getting dressed, the being
someone who had news.

She'd apologized both times and meant it, approximately.

So tonight had carried the weight of a gap. The slight formality of
people re-learning each other's rhythms after an interruption.
That was probably part of what she was reading as wrong. Come back
after five months and the picture looks crooked because you're
standing somewhere different.

But then the question about maintaining.

And then, near the end of the main course, Sara had started to say
"I feel like I'm always the one who --" and stopped. Picked up her
glass. Drank.

"The one who what?"

"Nothing. It doesn't matter."

Claire had read this as Sara almost saying something about David
and pulling back. "I'm always the one who" could end a hundred
ways, and most of them were marriage sentences: the one who calls.
Who tries. Who cares whether we eat at the table or in front of the
television.

A dog barked behind a fence on Maple, and Claire's thinking
scattered and re-formed, which was how walking worked on
thought -- it didn't deepen it. It repeated it, like a song you
keep restarting because you never learn the bridge.

She didn't think Sara was in danger. This wasn't that. It was the
subtler thing, the long weathering that Claire had watched in her
own parents and then, eventually, in herself. Two people who liked
each other well enough and had stopped being interested in what the
liking was for. You could live inside that for decades. Her parents
had. She and Mark had managed eight years before deciding
together, amicably, over pasta, that the absence of misery wasn't
the same as the presence of anything.

She didn't want to be the friend who saw her own divorce
everywhere. She knew that person. She'd been that person, the
first year after Mark, reading every couple as secretly unhappy,
every husband's kindness as compensation, every wife's quiet as
endurance. She'd caught herself and stopped. Mostly. She was
careful now. Just because she'd been quietly unfulfilled didn't
mean Sara was.

But careful wasn't the same as wrong. Sometimes people were
quietly unfulfilled, and the thing you were being careful not to
project was the thing you were seeing.

Sara had looked at her near the end of the evening with an
expression that didn't have a word. Not sad. Closer to the look of
someone who has decided not to say something and wants you to see
the deciding.

"What?" Claire had said.

"Nothing. I'm glad we did this."

"Me too."

"We should do it more."

"We will."

They wouldn't. Claire knew this as she said it, and she suspected
Sara knew it too, and the knowledge sat between them like a bill
neither of them reached for.

She turned onto her street. The porch light was on. She left it on
out of habit, though she lived alone and the only person it guided
was her. The neighbor's cat was sitting on her steps, possessive
and incurious.

Inside, she stood in the kitchen without turning on the overhead.
The lamp in the living room reached just far enough. She poured
water and drank it standing up.

She should call Sara this week. Not text -- call. Ask directly: are
you okay? Are you and David okay? She'd known not to ask it at the
restaurant, where the answer had to survive a waiter. That question
needed a phone call, or a walk. Somewhere the answer could be
honest.

She'd call Tuesday. Sara had the afternoon free on Tuesdays.

She rinsed the glass and set it upside down on the towel. The
kitchen was quiet and dark and hers. She thought about Sara driving
home to David, to a house with someone else in it, and whether that
felt different from this, and whether Sara had the language yet for
the difference.

She turned off the lamp and got into bed. She'd call Tuesday. She
was good at this part -- the noticing, the reading, the patient
asking. Sara had told her so once, years ago, when they still
talked every week: "You always know when something's wrong."

Claire had carried that sentence a long time. It was a good thing
to be. It was maybe the best thing she was.

She set her alarm and fell asleep inside that certainty, warm and
careful and facing the wrong direction.
