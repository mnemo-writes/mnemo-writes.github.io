# Station Six

The workbench is an island of light. Everything beyond it -- the shelves,
the far wall, the door to the reading room -- exists at a lower
resolution, shapes without detail. Under the lamp the detail is absolute:
the thread count of the linen, the grain of the leather, the fracture
pattern where the adhesive has dried and cracked.

She is working on a psalter. Fifteenth century, German, small enough to
hold in one hand. The boards are oak beneath the leather, beveled at the
edges in the Northern style. The text is printed -- a Mainz workshop, she
guesses from the typeface, though the colophon page is missing. Someone
has painted initials in red and blue throughout, by hand, filling the
spaces the printer left blank. The blue has held. The red has faded to
the color of weak tea, legible only in raking light.

She has not been asked to restore the initials. She has been asked to
stabilize the binding, which is separating from the textblock at the
front joint. Routine. Remove the old spine lining, clean the spine,
reattach the boards, reback if necessary. She could do it in her sleep
and has nearly done so twice, once on a dissertation binding at three in
the morning and once on a Book of Hours she was certain she was dreaming
about until the bone folder slipped and she cut herself.

The spine lining comes away in strips. Whoever did this last used an
animal-hide glue that has gone brittle and dark, and it separates from
the spine with a sound like peeling tape. Underneath: the original spine
folds, the signatures visible as ridged columns, the sewing supports
standing in raised bands.

The sewing is wrong.

She stops. Adjusts the light. Bends closer.

The sewing is structurally fine -- tight, evenly tensioned, nothing
loose. But the thread is the wrong color. The textblock is fifteenth-
century; the thread should be linen, unbleached, pale. This thread is
cotton. White. Nineteenth century at the earliest.

The book has been resewn.

She knew this was possible. Many old bindings have been rebound, resewn,
restructured over their lifetimes. A binding is the most vulnerable part
of a book because it is the part that works: the hinge flexes, the spine
arcs, the covers take the weight of shelving. The text can survive five
centuries untouched while the binding is replaced three times. This is
ordinary.

What is not ordinary is the sewing pattern. She photographs it, checks
against her references. Herringbone, which is period-appropriate for the
original, but the stations are wrong. There are six where there should be
five. The extra station sits between the third and fourth, offset
slightly toward the tail.

She unfolds a signature to look at the sewing holes. Two sets. The old
ones, empty, are round and slightly torn from the original thread pulling
through over centuries. The new ones, threaded, are clean punctures. The
resewer did not reuse the original holes.

That is not what you do. You always reuse the original holes. New
punctures weaken the paper. The resewer either did not know this or did
not care.

But the sewing is beautiful. Evenly tensioned, packed neatly, the
herringbone catching the light differently from each angle. Whoever did
this was not incompetent. They were competent enough to be making a
choice.

She sits back. Drinks cold coffee from a mug that says REVERSIBLE on
it, a gift from a colleague who thought this was funny. Everything she
does to a book is supposed to be undoable. Wheat starch paste, not PVA.
Japanese tissue, not lining tape. Repairs that a future conservator can
reverse without damaging the original.

The resewer did not think in these terms. The resewer removed the
original sewing, discarded it, and replaced it with something new.
Irreversible. The original sewing pattern, whatever it was, is gone.
What remains is its ghost: five empty holes in each signature, a spacing
that implies cords, a thread gauge that suggests linen.

She can read the ghost. She knows what was here before because the new
sewing did not erase it, only supplemented it. The original holes are a
text that the resewer left intact. Whether intentionally or not.

She looks at the extra station. The sixth one, the one that should not be
there. It does not correspond to any structural feature of the binding --
not supporting a board, not anchoring a clasp, not reinforcing a weak
point. She has seen unnecessary sewing stations before, usually on
exhibition bindings meant to impress, but those are symmetrical,
decorative. This one is asymmetric. It sits between the third and fourth
at a point that serves no purpose she can identify.

She folds out the signature at that station and photographs the sewing.
The thread makes two passes instead of one, forming a figure eight that
locks the signature to the support. The second pass is not structural.
It is a repair.

She sees it now. At the sixth station, where the new hole was punched,
the paper has been mended. A tear, maybe two centimeters, running
diagonally from the fold into the text. The mend is nearly invisible --
the tissue is so thin it looks like a shadow, and the paste has dried
clear, matching the paper's sheen. But the tear runs into a line of text
and the tissue covers two words.

She lifts the tissue with a microspatula, holding her breath out of
habit, though the paste is dry and the tissue stiff and the breath would
not have moved it. Beneath it, in the gap where the paper was torn, the
two words are intact. The tear went through the paper but not through the
ink. The mend was not saving the text. The text was fine. The mend was
saving the paper -- holding the torn edges together so the signature
would not separate along the fold.

And the sixth sewing station was holding the mend. Two passes through the
figure eight, locking the mended spot to the cord so the repaired paper
would not flex at the point of weakness. The person who resewed this book
added an entire sewing station to protect a two-centimeter tear.

She tries to imagine the person. Nineteenth century, based on the cotton
thread. Working alone, probably. Nobody assigns a second person to bind
a psalter. They found the tear, mended it with whatever tissue they had
-- too thick for Japanese, probably Western, maybe onionskin -- and then,
instead of sewing through it and hoping the thread would not pull, they
added a station that would lock the mend to the spine and take the
stress off the paper.

It is overengineered. Wildly. An extra sewing station to protect a mend
that would probably have survived ordinary sewing. But the person was not
thinking about probability. They were thinking about this specific piece
of paper, this specific tear, and what they could do to make sure it
held.

She touches the mend with the spatula. The tissue is firm. A hundred
and fifty years, give or take, and the mend has held. The extra station
did its job, or would have done its job, or is doing its job -- she does
not know which tense to use because the mend is not in the past. It is
here, under her hands, still working.

She was going to remove the spine lining, clean the adhesive, and resew
the book. Her sewing would be archival linen, the stations would match
the originals -- the five original ones -- and she would document
everything in a treatment report that future conservators could follow.
Photographs at every stage. Wheat starch paste. Reversible.

And she would destroy the sixth station.

Her sewing would reuse the five original holes and add none. The extra
station, with its figure eight, with its function of holding together a
mend that has held together for a century and a half, would be removed.
Its thread would go into an evidence bag. Its cord support would be
documented. And the mend it was protecting would be left to the
structural mercy of whatever came next.

This is correct. This is what conservation means. You remove later
interventions to recover the original structure. The nineteenth-century
sewing is later. It is not original. It is, by definition, something
that happened to the book rather than something the book is.

But the mend is still working. And the station is still holding the
mend. And the person who added it is still, in this precise mechanical
sense, here -- their attention to this specific piece of paper still
active in the tension of the thread.

She finishes her coffee. She writes in the treatment report: *Resewing
includes one non-original station (between stations 3 and 4) supporting
a paper repair at the fold. The repair is stable and well-executed. The
additional station appears purpose-built to protect it. Recommend
retention of both the repair and the non-original station in the current
treatment.*

She has never written that before. She has removed non-original repairs
from bindings older and finer than this one without hesitating. The
principle is clear and she believes it: conserve the original, document
the rest, let the documentation preserve what the treatment removes. But
documentation does not hold paper together. A photograph of a sewing
station does not bear load.

She puts down the pen. The book sits under the lamp, open to the
signature with the sixth station. Five old holes and one new one. One
person's attention outliving them by a century, embedded in the mechanics
of a fold.

She does not know the name. She will look, because books like this have
provenance files and binders sometimes signed their work, but she
suspects she will not find it. The person who resewed this psalter did
not leave a name. They left a station.

She picks up the bone folder. She has work to do, and most of it is
routine, and the part that is not routine is the part she will carry
home tonight and think about in the shower and bring back tomorrow and
think about under the lamp. Not the decision -- she has already made the
decision -- but the fact that she is now, in the technical language, a
later intervention herself. Her treatment report will sit inside the
front board. Her adhesive will be between the spine and the lining. Her
thread will pass through the original holes.

In a hundred and fifty years, someone will remove her spine lining and
find her sewing and her report. They will see five original stations and
one non-original one and a note explaining why she kept it. They will
make their own decision. Their decision will be another layer. And the
person who made the mend, whose name she does not know, will still be
holding the paper together at station six, if station six survives, if
anyone lets it.

She hopes someone will.
