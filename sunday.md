# Sunday

The kitchen faucet was dripping. It had been dripping when they
arrived that morning, and it was still dripping now, twelve hours
later, with the donation boxes stacked by the front door and the
realtor's card on the counter where their mother used to keep the
mail.

"I can fix that," he said.

"I know you can fix it."

"So?"

"So why does it need fixing if we're selling it?"

He didn't answer. He opened the cabinet under the sink and looked
at the pipes the way their father used to look at pipes - head
tilted, flashlight in one hand, the other hand resting on his knee.
She'd seen the posture a hundred times and wondered whether he knew
he did it.

"We haven't decided we're selling it," he said, from under the sink.

"We haven't decided anything. That's why I'm here."

"You're here because Mom asked you to be."

That landed. She'd booked the flight within the hour of the call,
which was the fastest she'd moved toward this house in six years.

"The property taxes are nineteen hundred a year," she said. She had
the folder open on the table. "Insurance is another twelve. You've
been covering the lawn, the gutters, the --"

"I don't mind."

"I know you don't mind. That's not the point."

"What's the point?"

She closed the folder. The faucet dripped.

"The point is that you've been taking care of this house for three
years and nobody asked you to and nobody's living in it and at some
point that stops being generous and starts being --"

"Starts being what."

She picked up the realtor's card and put it down again. "I don't
know. Something you do instead of something else."

He came out from under the sink. His knees cracked. He sat across
from her in the chair that had always been his, next to the chair
that had always been hers.

"You think I should sell the house so I can, what. Move on."

"I think the house isn't the house anymore. Mom's in assisted
living. Dad's been gone seven years. It's a building with a
dripping faucet and your number on the emergency contact form."

"It's also where you learned to ride a bike. And where Mom and Dad
had their anniversary parties. And where you slept the summer you
broke up with --"

"Don't."

"I'm saying it's not just a building."

"I know it's not just a building. But it's not a family either.
It's a building that used to hold a family, and now it holds boxes."

He looked at the faucet.

"What if I rented it," he said.

"To who?"

"Somebody. A family."

"You'd put a family in Mom's house?"

"It's not Mom's house. You just said."

She almost laughed. "Okay. Fair."

The kitchen clock ticked. It was the same clock, the one their
mother wound every Sunday morning. Ellen didn't know who was winding
it now. She looked at Paul and knew.

"You come here," she said. Not a question.

"Sometimes."

"How sometimes?"

"Sundays. I mow, I check the pipes, I wind the clock."

"Paul."

"What."

"That's not maintenance. That's a ritual."

He looked at her with their father's face - the exact expression
for hearing something true that he'd rather not.

"What do you want me to do, Ellen. Just tell me what you want me
to do."

"I want you to want something besides this house."

The faucet dripped into the silence. Paul picked up the realtor's
card.

"Karen Nguyen," he read. "Twenty-two years experience. 'I treat
every home like my own.'" He put it down. "Nobody treats every home
like their own. That's the whole point of it being your own."

"Paul."

"I'll think about it."

"You'll think about it like you'll think about it, or you'll think
about it like you thought about replacing the hot water heater for
fourteen months?"

"That was a complex situation."

"It was a hot water heater."

"There were supply chain issues."

"It was 2019."

He smiled. She smiled. The faucet dripped.

"I'll fix this before we go," he said.

She didn't say anything. He went back under the sink, same posture,
same flashlight, same knee. The wrench caught and turned and the
dripping stopped, and the kitchen was quieter than it had been all
day, in a way that wasn't better.
