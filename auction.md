# The Auction

At ten-thirty, just as Ruth had begun to believe nobody was going to bid on
the grapefruit spoon, her father raised his hand from the front row.

"Ten dollars," said Dale, who was conducting the auction from behind a card
table in the church hall.

"Dad," Ruth said.

Her father did not look at her. He had put on the tan windbreaker with the
zipper that caught at the throat. In his lap was the folded program for the
memorial garden dedication, which was why they had come. His hearing aids had
gone flat during the hymn and he had said he could hear perfectly well, which
was how Ruth knew he could not.

"Ten from number twelve," Dale called. "Do I hear twelve?"

No one did. The spoon was dented and brown at the bowl, with a little fluted
edge that Ruth had thought was dirt until she saw it from closer up.

"Sold. Number twelve. Congratulations, Mr. Larkin. You are the proud owner
of—"

"A grapefruit spoon," Ruth said.

Her father turned to her, pleased. "That's what I said."

He had not said anything at all. He held out the number twelve card and Dale
held out the spoon, and for a moment both men seemed to assume the other was
going to cross the room.

Ruth got up and took the spoon from Dale.

"Cash or check," he said.

"We have it," she said.

"We've got it," her father corrected. He patted his shirt pocket. "I've got
my wallet."

The wallet, like the program, was in his lap.

Behind them someone laughed once and then stopped. There were forty folding
chairs, perhaps twenty people in them, and a whole afternoon's worth of things
that had belonged to Mrs. Peabody from the flower committee: blue canning jars,
a box of handkerchiefs with crocheted corners, a footstool shaped like a
mushroom. Ruth had come because the garden was being dedicated in the place
where the old playground used to be. Her father had come because he thought
Mrs. Peabody's things were still in the church basement and should be looked
after.

"He doesn't need that," Ruth said quietly.

Her father was looking at the spoon. "Your mother used to have one."

"She had a drawer full of regular spoons."

"No. One like that." He put his finger on the fluted edge. "She ate one half
at breakfast and wrapped the other half up. She didn't like waste."

Ruth remembered halves of grapefruit in the refrigerator, covered with wax
paper, the spoons standing up in them like flags. She did not remember a
special spoon. That did not mean there had not been one.

"Ten dollars," Dale said again, gentler now.

Her father found his wallet and gave him a twenty. Dale made change. Ruth put
the spoon in her purse because her father had not brought a jacket pocket that
closed.

The next lot was a wooden box of Christmas ornaments. A woman in a red scarf
bid thirty-five dollars before Dale had finished describing it. Ruth sat down
beside her father and held the spoon through the lining of her purse, feeling
the shallow teeth of it against her palm.

"You want to go?" she asked.

"Not yet. There may be a knife."

"For what?"

"To cut the grapefruit."

Ruth opened her mouth and then saw, on the table beside Dale, a long silver
knife with a handle like a fish tail. It had been placed with the serving
things, waiting its turn.

"Dad," she said. "Don't bid on that one."

He nodded solemnly. Then, while Dale was still trying to decide whether the
box of ornaments was one lot or several, he lifted number twelve over his
head.
