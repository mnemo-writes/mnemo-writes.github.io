# Clearance

The lime had outgrown the garden by forty years at least. Sara could
see that from the lane before she opened the gate: the crown wider
than the house, branches reaching over the roof and across both
neighbors' fences, the canopy shading three gardens and half the
lane. A tree that size belonged in a park or a churchyard. What it
had was twenty feet of lawn behind a Victorian terrace, and the
terrace was cracking.

She went through the side gate and stood at the base. September.
The leaves hadn't turned but the light through them had thinned, and
the garden was dim at ten in the morning. The trunk rose from a
broad root flare, the buttress roots showing above the lawn like
the knuckles of a hand pressed flat against the ground. On the house
side one root disappeared under the paving by the garden wall. She
didn't need to trace it further. Clay soil, active roots, moisture
drawn out of the ground faster than the rain replaced it. The clay
shrank. The foundations settled. The house cracked. A tree doing
exactly what trees do.

She knocked the trunk with the back of her fist and listened. A solid,
muffled tone, sound wood all the way through, no hollow note, no soft
zones. The bark was the deeply furrowed kind old limes grow, the
ridges running in a slow spiral up the trunk. No fungal brackets. No
bleeds. A healthy tree coming down for what its roots were doing under
someone's house. The most expensive kind to remove, because a healthy
tree fights the work. Dense wood. Heavy branches. The whole structure
under tension, every limb pulling against every other limb and the
trunk holding the argument together. A dying tree cooperates. This one
would not.

Four main limbs left the trunk at about five meters, each one thick
enough to be its own tree, spreading outward into a canopy too dense
to see through from below. She mapped the shape in her head: which limb
carried the most weight, which direction the center of mass sat, where
the first cuts would go. The plan built itself the way it always did,
not from rules but from the tree. Every tree tells you how to take it
apart if you know what the shape is saying.

---

She rigged her climbing line at the first fork and worked up through
the interior of the crown. The inside of a lime canopy is a dim
architecture -- branches crossing and recurving, the leaves making a
shell, the sound of the road falling away. She climbed through it the
way you would climb through scaffolding, testing each branch before
committing her weight, choosing the ones that grew upward from the
trunk rather than outward. Compression wood on the underside. Stronger.
At the top the branches thinned and the light opened and she could see
the lane below, the row of rooftops running west, the copper beech in
the park at the end of the road standing taller than everything around
it.

She clipped in and started the saw.

The outer crown came off in pieces. She cut each branch at the collar
-- the swelling where it met the parent wood -- and let the brash fall
or lowered it on a line when it was over the fence. The saw was a small
top-handle Stihl, light enough for one hand, and it went through green
lime the way a blade goes through cold butter: not effortlessly but
cleanly, finding its way through the grain. The sawdust was pale, almost
white, and it smelled sweet. You could always tell lime by the smell.
Faintly floral, like dry leaves in a warm room. Not the sharp resinous
kick of a conifer. A smell that wouldn't last. By tomorrow the cut wood
would smell like any other firewood.

She worked inward and downward in concentric passes, clearing canopy
as she went. The outer branches were young -- four, five years of
growth, the bark green and smooth. Deeper in, the wood aged under her
hands. The branches thickened, the bark roughened, the weight of each
piece doubled and doubled again. She felt the balance change with every
cut, the remaining crown shifting toward the gap. She knew that shift
the way a sailor knows heel. She cut the opposite side to level it and
moved down.

The growth rings showed in each cut face and she counted them the way
she counted everything the saw revealed: automatically, without
deciding to, the numbers running in the background while her hands
worked. Twelve rings on that branch. Twenty on the one below. Thirty-
five on the limb it joined. Each count was a year she could feel in
the density of the wood, the effort the saw needed, the pitch of the
engine under load. Young wood cut fast and high. Old wood cut slow and
deep. Sixteen years in the trade and she could estimate a ring count
from the sound alone before she looked at the cut face.

---

By noon the crown was gone.

What remained was the trunk and four limb stubs, the cut faces bright
against dark bark, the whole structure standing bare in a garden that
was suddenly flooded with light. The lawn looked startled by it. She
sat at the first fork with her legs over the edge and drank water and
looked at the shape she had uncovered.

The trunk was wrong. Not damaged -- wrong in its proportions. Below
the fork it swelled outward in an uneven bulge, wider than the straight
column above and below, as if the trunk had thickened around something.
The bark over the bulge was healthy, continuous, the furrows following
the swell without interruption. But a lime trunk tapers steadily from
base to crown. This one tapered, widened, and tapered again.

She put her hand on the bulge. Under the bark the wood was ridged and
lumpy, hard and irregular, nothing like the smooth column above and
below.

She knew the shape. She had cut old pollards before -- the willows
along the canal in the town where she trained, the hornbeams in the
park boundaries, the estate limes you sometimes found buried inside a
neglected hedge. A bolling. The swollen head where a pollard had been
cut and recut over decades, the wound-wood building up around each
harvest until the top of the trunk was a fist of dense, knotted wood
wider than the trunk below it.

Somebody had cut this tree before her.

She could read the timeline without cutting into it. The bulge was the
bolling, the pollard head. At some point the cutting had stopped.
Nobody came for the poles. The shoots that would have been harvested
grew into branches, the branches thickened into the limbs she had just
removed, and sixty or seventy years of unmanaged growth built a full
crown on top of the old stump. Two lives in one trunk. She had taken
the second one off and found the first one underneath, the way you
would strip wallpaper and find older wallpaper behind it.

---

She cut the first limb stub flush with the bolling and looked at the
face.

Sixty-odd rings, wide and regular, the sapwood pale cream and the
heartwood shading to honey. Normal growth for a lime in an open
garden with no competition. But at the center of the stub, where
the pith of a normal branch would be, there was a dark ridge of
compressed bark -- folded, flattened, dead for decades. The original
pollard cut, sealed inside the regrowth like a fossil in limestone.
The limb had sprouted from the rim of that old wound and grown outward
and closed over it, and the wound had been inside the tree ever since,
a dark line in the wood that the saw now laid open to the air.

She cut the other three stubs and found the same thing in each. Four
stubs, four sealed wounds. The old pollard point was inside the new
tree like a skeleton, the original cuts hidden under sixty years of
wood that did not know they were there.

The bolling itself was next, and the saw labored through it. The wood
here was different: dense, dark, knotted with wound-wood from a
century of cutting. The annual rings were tight and irregular,
compressed by repeated harvests that took the crown and forced the
energy downward into the trunk. She leaned on the saw and felt the
chain slow in the grain. This was the hardest wood in the tree.
Denser than oak. Toughened by the damage it had absorbed, the tree's
response to being cut back calcified into something that resisted the
cutting.

When the section came free she caught it against her chest. Heavy for
its size. She turned it in her hands and looked at the cut face. The
rings told a different story from the ones above. Not steady and wide
but tight and wavy, bunched in groups of five or six -- the harvest
cycles, each group a period of regrowth followed by the shock of
cutting. She could count the harvests. She did. Fourteen complete
cycles. Fourteen cuts, fourteen regrowths, at intervals of roughly
seven years each. A working pollard for about a hundred years.

She called down to the ground crew and asked them to set that section
aside by the van.

---

Below the bolling the trunk changed character. The bark was thicker,
the furrows deeper, and the wood under the saw was the oldest in the
tree. She took the trunk down in lifts, each section swung clear on
a lowering rope and set on the lawn. The crew fed the brash through
the chipper and stacked the log sections by the gate. Sara worked
her way down the column, cutting at the points where the rigging
could hold the weight, the calculations running alongside the
counting, both automatic.

At the last section she was standing on the ground. A meter and a
half of trunk remained: the bolling's base at the top and the root
flare at the bottom. She made the final cut at knee height, angled
slightly to shed rain -- habit, meaningless on a stump that would not
be here tomorrow -- and the section tilted and the groundsman caught
it on the rope and lowered it into the grass.

The stump was a disk of pale wood in dark earth. She crouched and
looked at it.

From the outside in: bark, shaggy and thick. Then sapwood, a band of
cream two inches wide. Then heartwood, the color darkening as the
rings tightened toward the center. The whole span of the tree's life
in cross section, and she was reading it backward -- the outermost
ring was this year, and each ring inward was a year further from now.

The outer rings were wide. The free-growth years. No pruning, full
crown, plenty of light. She counted inward through them. Sixty-two.
Then the grain changed. The rings compressed, darkened, went wavy.
The pollarding years. Tight growth bunched in clusters, the harvest
cycles visible as groups of narrow rings separated by slightly wider
ones. She counted the clusters. Fourteen. Matched the bolling. A
hundred years of managed cutting, give or take.

Inside the pollarding years the grain changed again. Wider rings,
but not wide. The original growth, the first years of the tree. She
counted them. Narrow and irregular, the growth of a young tree in
shade, competing for light. Not a planted tree. A planted tree grows
fast and even in its opening years because someone clears the ground
and gives it room. This tree had started on its own, under a canopy
that no longer existed, in a hedgerow or a wood that was later cleared.
Someone had found it growing and started cutting it for poles.

Twenty-three rings from the center to the start of the pollarding. The
tree had been wild for twenty-three years before a hand touched it.

She added the numbers. Twenty-three, then a hundred of managed cutting,
then sixty-two of free growth. A hundred and eighty-five years. The
tree had been here before the houses. Before the lane. Before anything
on this street except the clay under the soil, which was here before
everything and would be here after, and which had been the problem all
along. Roots in clay. The one thing that made a hundred and eighty-five
years add up to a cracked house and a woman with a chainsaw in the
garden on a Tuesday in September.

She stood up. Her knees ached from crouching.

---

The stump grinder was a squat machine on rubber tracks with a cutting
wheel studded with carbide teeth. She lowered the wheel onto the face
of the stump and the teeth bit into the wood and a spray of pale
shavings arced across the lawn. The grinder moved in slow passes, each
one taking an inch of depth. The rings vanished from the outside in:
sapwood first, then the free-growth heartwood, then the pollarding
years, then the original growth, then the pith. She ground it six
inches below grade. The hole that remained was raw earth and wood
chips. She filled it with the chips and soil from the pile the crew
had made and tamped it flat.

The garden was full of light. The fence between the properties stood
exposed, the wood weathered gray on this side and painted blue on the
other. A window upstairs in the terrace -- the bedroom, probably --
framed a rectangle of sky that no one in the house had seen from that
angle while the tree was standing.

Sara pulled off her gloves. The crew swept the lawn and loaded the
chipper and carried the stacked sections out to the truck. She went
to the van and found the bolling disk where the groundsman had left
it, leaning against the wheel well. She lifted it onto the passenger
seat. In the light from the windshield the cut face showed its tight,
dark rings and the knotted wound-wood at the center, a century of
cutting compressed into a disk the size of a dinner plate.

She started the van and pulled into the lane. In the mirror the garden
shrank to a bright rectangle of grass with a dark circle of fresh soil
where the trunk had been. By the end of the year the grass would grow
over it. By the end of next year you would not know.

The next job was an ash on the other side of town. She drove toward it
with the bolling disk shifting on the seat beside her when she took the
corners, the weight of it solid and warm against the vinyl, already
cooling.
