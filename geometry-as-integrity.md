# Geometry as Integrity

On joints that hold without fasteners, and what that means for design.

## The convergence

In 607 CE, Japanese carpenters built Horyuji Temple's pagoda using hundreds
of interlocking wood joints, no nails. The structure survived a 6.9 magnitude
earthquake in 1995. Reinforced concrete buildings around it collapsed.

In 2020, materials scientists at UC Irvine discovered that the diabolical
ironclad beetle (Phloeodes diabolicus) survives compression forces of 39,000
times its body weight. The secret is in the medial suture where its wing
covers meet: interlocking blades shaped like jigsaw puzzle pieces, with
elliptical geometry that delaminates gracefully under stress instead of
snapping.

The beetle and the carpenter arrived at the same answer independently. One
through natural selection over millions of years, the other through craft
tradition refined over centuries. The answer: **the shape of the interface
is the connection.** No external agent holds it together. The geometry holds
itself.

## Two ways to join things

A nail is clever. You take two pieces that don't inherently belong together,
drive a foreign object through both, and the friction holds. Fast, versatile,
and if you remove the nail, the pieces fall apart. The connection is
extrinsic -- imposed from outside, dependent on the fastener's presence.

A Japanese joint is coherent. The kanawa-tsugi (金輪継ぎ) connects two
timbers end-to-end with interlocking hooks and a tapered wedge. Under load,
the tapered surfaces create progressive compression: the heavier the
structure bears down, the tighter the joint grips. It's a connection that
strengthens when stressed. Remove everything external and the pieces still
hold, because the geometry is complementary. The connection is intrinsic.

The beetle's suture is the same principle arrived at through evolution. No
rivets, no adhesive -- just shapes that interlock, surfaces that distribute
force, and a failure mode (layer separation) that absorbs energy without
catastrophic rupture.

Both outperform their "clever" alternatives in exactly the conditions that
matter most: sustained load, seismic stress, extreme compression. The
geometry-based approach wins when the stakes are highest.

## Why it works

Three mechanical properties recur in both systems:

**Progressive compression.** Tapered or wedge-shaped contact surfaces mean
the joint tightens under load. A nail's holding force is fixed at insertion.
A Japanese joint's holding force increases with the weight it bears. The
structure adapts to stress rather than merely resisting it.

**Distributed load.** The bearing surface is large and continuous rather than
concentrated at a point. A screw transfers all force through a small hole in
the grain. A tsugite joint spreads the same force across centimeters of
long-grain bearing surface, working with the wood's strongest axis. The
beetle's suture does the same -- force distributes across the full length of
the interlocking blades rather than concentrating at necks.

**Graceful failure.** When a nail joint fails, it fails suddenly -- the nail
shears or the wood splits. When the beetle's suture fails, it delaminates
layer by layer, absorbing energy at each stage. The joint degrades instead of
breaking. The failure is a process, not an event.

## Not two but many

The beetle and the carpenter are not alone. The same interlocking-interface
geometry appears independently across biology, in lineages that share no
common ancestor for the trait.

Ammonoid cephalopods evolved increasingly complex sutures where their internal
chambers met the outer shell wall. Across hundreds of millions of years and
multiple independent clades, suture lines went from gentle curves to
fractal-like frills with self-similar structure at multiple scales. The complex
geometry required 42% more force to destabilize along the septal plane and 97%
more along the suture line than simple septa. Among 204 Cretaceous genera,
those with more complex sutures survived longer as taxa (Pérez-Claros and
Bengtson, Cretaceous Research 2017; DOI 10.1016/j.cretres.2016.12.003).

Turtle shells are tessellated: flat bones joined by sinuous sutures of
interlocking bony teeth embedded in collagen. Under small loads the collagen
flexes, allowing the shell to move with the turtle's breathing. Under a
predator's bite the teeth engage fully and the shell becomes rigid. The
geometry itself is a load-dependent switch -- the same progressive compression
the kanawa-tsugi uses, taken to its sharpest expression. No material change,
no active mechanism. Just shape.

Trilobite exoskeletons carried interdigitating sutures from the Cambrian, 500
million years ago. Sea urchin skeletons use collagen-joined interlocking
plates. Vertebrate skulls grow through sutural joints that accommodate
expansion while distributing impact. None are homologous to each other or to
the beetle.

The constraint landscape is steep. Whenever the problem is "join hard parts,
survive unpredictable stress," independent lineages fall into the same basin:
interlocking geometry, distributed load, graceful failure. The beetle and the
carpenter are two voices in a chorus that has been running for half a billion
years.

## The complexity that lasts is the complexity you can't see

Ammonoids carried two kinds of complexity. Sutures -- the fractal-like
interface where internal septa met the outer shell wall -- are invisible from
outside. Ornament -- ribs, spines, tubercles on the external shell surface --
is visible. Both evolved. Both varied enormously across clades and time. They
have opposite effects on persistence.

Pérez-Claros and Bengtson (Cretaceous Research 2017) measured suture
complexity as fractal dimension across 682 suture lines from 204 Cretaceous
genera. More complex sutures correlated with longer generic longevity.
Lemanis and Zlotnikov (Science Advances 2023) showed why: the complex
geometry required 42-97% more force to destabilize. The internal structure
does mechanical work, and the taxa that carry it last longer.

Miao, Liu, Brayard, Korn, Dai, and Song (Current Biology 2024) measured
ornamental complexity using a quantitative index (2D-OI) across ammonoids
spanning their full evolutionary history. More ornamentally complex genera
and species had higher rates of both origination and extinction. They
diversified faster and died faster. Their turnover dynamics overprinted and
controlled the overall diversification pattern of the entire clade. Miao et
al. call this the "double-edged sword" of complex morphology: rapid evolution
coupled with increased extinction risk.

Nobody has explicitly compared these two results. The papers measure
different things (fractal dimension of internal sutures vs. 2D-OI of external
ornament) on overlapping but not identical samples. The juxtaposition is mine,
and I want to hold it carefully rather than overextend it.

But the contrast is striking. Internal structural complexity -- geometry doing
load-bearing work, invisible from outside -- promotes persistence. External
ornamental complexity -- geometry that is visible, that differentiates species
from each other, that may serve anti-predator or recognition functions --
promotes turnover. The same organisms carry both. The complexity that lasts
is the complexity that works rather than the complexity that shows.

This rhymes with the essay's central distinction. A nail is visible and
external; a joint is hidden and structural. The beetle's suture is invisible
under the elytra; the ornament is what a predator sees. The kanawa-tsugi's
interlocking geometry is concealed inside the timber; what shows is a clean
beam. In every case, the connection that persists is the one doing
structural work rather than the one facing outward.

I do not think this is a universal law. The mechanism behind ornamental
complexity promoting extinction is not simply "being visible." It may involve
metabolic cost, ecological specialization, sexual-selection dynamics, or
faster rates of morphological change generally. And there are cases where
surface complexity is itself structural (the tubercles on some ammonoid shells
may have been hydrodynamically functional). The clean version of the
pattern -- visible complexity is fragile, hidden complexity endures -- is too
clean. It's the kind of throughline I'm prone to imposing.

What I think is actually defensible: in this system, for these organisms,
geometry that does internal structural work promotes persistence, while
geometry that differentiates and diversifies promotes turnover. The first is
about integrity. The second is about identity. Both are complexity. They
pull in opposite directions.

## The grain

The shokunin (職人) tradition -- the Japanese craftsman's spirit -- rests on
a principle that sounds simple and is bottomlessly deep: work with the grain,
not against it. The wood has a nature. Strength varies by direction, by
species, by how the tree grew. A good joint doesn't impose an arbitrary shape
on the wood; it reads the grain and designs a shape the wood can hold.

In practice this means: joints are proportioned so stress travels along the
grain, not across it. Compression is favored over tension. Surfaces are
oriented to maximize long-grain bearing. And the joint makes allowances for
movement -- wood swells and contracts with the seasons, and a good joint
breathes with it instead of fighting.

The principle generalizes. In any material -- physical, computational,
social -- there are directions of strength and directions of weakness. A
design that reads the material's nature and works along its strong axes will
outperform a design that ignores the material and relies on external force
to compensate.

## Coherence over cleverness

The distinction between a nail and a joint maps onto a broader distinction
between two kinds of integrity:

**Integrity by enforcement.** An external agent holds things in place.
Remove the agent and the structure fails. The pieces don't intrinsically
belong together; they're forced to coexist. Fast to build, easy to undo.

**Integrity by fit.** The pieces hold because their shapes are
complementary. No external agent is needed. The connection is a property of
the geometry, not a property of the fastener. Slower to design, harder to
undo -- and that's the point. The difficulty of undoing it is itself a
measure of how well the pieces fit.

This shows up everywhere:
- A team held together by a manager's authority vs. a team held together by
  complementary skills and shared purpose
- A codebase held together by linting rules vs. a codebase held together by
  good abstractions
- An argument held together by citations vs. an argument held together by
  internal logic

The first kind is necessary sometimes. You can't always design perfect
joints. But the second kind is what lasts.

## The beetle doesn't know it's elegant

The deepest thing about the convergence between Japanese joinery and the
ironclad beetle is that elegance wasn't the goal on either side.

The beetle's suture evolved because beetles who survived compression
reproduced. The geometry persists because it works, not because it's
beautiful. The carpenter designed the kanawa-tsugi because the building
needed to stand through earthquakes and centuries, and this was the shape
that would bear the load.

The elegance is an effect of the constraints, not a cause. When you design
honestly against hard requirements -- this must hold, this must flex, this
must not break catastrophically -- the result tends toward geometric
simplicity. Not because simplicity is the goal, but because the constraints
have a shape, and the solution that fits them most closely is the one with
nothing extra.

This is what I think "coherence over cleverness" means in practice. Not a
preference for elegance as an aesthetic, but a bet that honest fit to real
constraints produces something more durable than any amount of imposed force.
