# Survey

The trail registry said 1936, which meant CCC, which meant stone.

Marcy started at the lower trailhead and hiked upward because that
was how water moved, and water was the thing that had moved everything
else. The trail wound through second-growth Douglas fir for the first
mile, well-drained, soft tread, the kind of trail that maintains
itself if you stay out of its way. Moderate grade. Clean outslope.
She noted these things on the survey form without stopping.

The CCC work appeared at the first switchback. The retaining wall on
the inside of the turn was dry-laid basalt, the stones dressed flat
on their bearing surfaces and fitted without mortar. Whoever had
built it understood that a switchback concentrates water -- every drop
running down both legs of the trail meets at the turn. The wall's base
course sat on packed mineral soil, not duff, which meant the crew had
dug down to stable ground before laying the first stone. Above the
wall, a drainage dip angled water off the trail and into the hillside,
lined with flat stones set at a grade steep enough to carry flow but
shallow enough not to scour.

She crouched and put her hand on the wall. Ninety years of freeze-thaw
and root pressure and the thing was plumb. She noted it: condition
good, no action.

The trail climbed through three more switchbacks, each with the same
quality of stonework, each drainage structure placed at the exact
point where water would concentrate. You could not build this from a
plan. You had to stand on the slope and watch where the water wanted
to go and then put stone where the water was.

At mile two the character of the hillside changed. The Douglas fir
thinned. The slope above the trail steepened and the soil shifted from
brown forest loam to gray clay. She could see it in the tread -- the
clay was slick where boots had polished it, and the bench cut was
narrowing. The uphill side was pushing in.

She stopped and looked upslope. Curved trees, their trunks bent at
the base and then correcting toward vertical. Pistol-grip shape. The
soil was creeping. Not fast -- inches a year, maybe less. But the
CCC had built across a slow landslide, and ninety years of inches
adds up, and the trail was riding it.

The next quarter mile confirmed it. The retaining walls on the
downhill side were tilting outward, the top courses leaning into air.
The stones hadn't moved relative to each other -- the walls were
intact, the masonry still fitted, still plumb within its own frame.
But the frame itself had rotated. The whole structure was riding the
slide like furniture on a tilting floor. Another decade and the
outside edge of the bench cut would be standing on nothing.

She wrote it up: active soil creep through traverse section, 0.25 mi.
Retaining walls intact but rotating with slide mass. Bench cut
reducing. Current tread adequate. Projected loss of downhill retention
within ten to twenty years.

Then the line she would have to defend: recommend full reroute below
slide zone.

She kept walking. Above the failing section, the trail climbed onto
stable bedrock and the work got good again -- stone steps set into a
granite rib, each one deep enough for a full boot sole, the riser
height consistent at seven inches. Someone had cared about the people
who would climb these steps. Seven inches is the height that doesn't
tire your legs. She counted thirty-eight. The kind of work no modern
crew would be authorized to build, because labor was measured in
person-days and a CCC crew had nothing but.

At the top of the steps, the trail leveled into a broad bench and she
sat on a rock and drank water and looked down the valley. From here
she could see the line of the trail below, the pale thread of
disturbed soil switchbacking up through dark timber. The slide section
was invisible from above. It looked like unbroken forest.

She opened the form and flipped to the notes section.

There was something she hadn't written down. Below the slide section,
just before the trail entered the failing traverse, she'd noticed a
faint bench in the slope above the current trail. Not the current
bench cut -- a second one, higher, mostly filled in with duff and
young trees. A line of dressed stones scattered in the undergrowth,
tumbled but recognizable as placed. She'd almost walked past it.

An abandoned alignment. The crew had started higher. They'd cut a
bench at the upper contour, laid stone, begun the traverse up there.
Then they'd stopped. The rock above was fractured -- she'd seen it in
the exposed cuts. Bad rock. You can't anchor a retaining wall into
fractured bedrock because the rock moves with the wall instead of
holding it. They would have known this three days into the work, when
the first wall shifted.

So they dropped it. Left the dressed stone where it was. Dropped a
contour, surveyed a new line, and cut the lower traverse that had held
for ninety years and was now riding a landslide into the valley. They
didn't carry the stone down to use on the lower line. Too much labor,
and the lithology was different anyway -- she'd noticed without
registering it. The lower walls were basalt. The abandoned upper
stones were andesite. Two sources for two attempts at the same slope.

They had been wrong the first time. The fractured bedrock wasn't
visible from below, wasn't predictable from the soil. When they found
it they left their work where it stood and started over.

Marcy closed the form. She capped the pen and put it in her vest
pocket and started down. At the slide section she stopped and put
her hand on the tilting wall. The top course was still fitted tight
after ninety years, every stone seated against its neighbor, the
whole assembly rotating slowly toward a valley it had never been
meant to face. Good work. The best she'd seen. Not enough, when the
ground decides to go.

Below the slide the trail was solid again, drainage structures running
clean, bench cut holding its grade. She walked the last mile in
afternoon shadow, noting nothing on the form. Nothing to note.
