# Pale

Marta had been sitting with the passage since ten o'clock, which meant five hours
and the better part of a pot of tea, and the passage had not moved.

The problem was a single word. *Szarzyzna.* In the novel - Wrona's novel, the one
Marta had translated seventeen years ago and which had earned her a modest
reputation and the lasting displeasure of two colleagues who would have translated
it differently - szarzyzna appears in a scene where a woman named Basia sits in her
kitchen at four in the afternoon in November. Wrona gives the kitchen in careful
detail: the vinyl tablecloth with its pattern of small blue flowers, the clock
above the stove that runs three minutes fast, the radiator under the window that
clicks twice before it comes on. And then: *Szarzyzna lying across the table like
an extra cloth.*

Marta had translated it as: *The pale gray light lying across the table like an
extra cloth.*

This was fine. It was what the word meant. It was the kind of translation you could
defend in a seminar and no one could object, and Marta had defended it exactly once,
at a panel in Chicago where a graduate student had proposed "dullness" instead and
Marta had explained, patiently, that dullness imported a judgment the narrator
didn't make. Basia is not judging the light. She's sitting in it.

But "the pale gray light" was five words for one word, and three of those five were
wrong.

Not wrong. Thin. "Pale" described the brightness. "Gray" described the color.
"Light" named the referent. Together they made a description where the Polish made a
recognition. Szarzyzna wasn't a description of what the light looked like. It was
the name for the condition of being in it - the particular grayness of a late
afternoon in November in a Polish kitchen where the radiator hasn't come on yet and
the day, which was never particularly bright, is finishing the only way it knows
how.

Marta took a sip of cold tea and looked at her notepad. She had tried:

*The szarzyzna across the table like an extra cloth.*

This was surrender dressed as strategy. She crossed it out.

*The November gray lying across the table.*

Better, in that it recovered the temporal dimension - it was specifically November,
not just any gray. But worse in that it made the season an adjective, which directed
the reader's attention to when, when the Polish wasn't directing attention at all. In
the Polish, szarzyzna was not pointing at anything. It was already there.

*The graying of the table like an extra cloth.*

No. This turned atmosphere into process. Nothing was graying. The gray was
established. It had been established since the first sentence of the chapter, which
opened: *Basia had been sitting in the kitchen since the morning, or maybe longer.*
The gray was the ground, not the event.

Marta put the pen down and looked out the window. Her own afternoon was thinning.
The yard was bronze and dry, mid-September rather than November, but the angle was
sympathetic - the sun low enough that the shadows of the hedgerow reached the house.
She would lose the desk light in twenty minutes, maybe thirty.

She turned back to the Polish text. What was Wrona doing with the sentence? Not
describing Basia's kitchen - the prior paragraph had done that, with the tablecloth
and the clock and the radiator. The details were precise and domestic and
unremarkable. Any Polish reader over forty would recognize that kitchen. The
tablecloth pattern, the fast clock, the radiator - these were not Basia's kitchen,
they were the kitchen, the generic kitchen underlying every specific one, and Wrona
built it with details specific enough to trigger recognition without being
distinctive enough to particularize.

And then szarzyzna. Not another detail. The atmosphere that all the details sit in.
The word that says: you have been in this room. You know this gray. You are
remembering, not imagining.

How do you translate a memory the reader doesn't have?

Marta had written her original translation in the spring of 2009, in this same room,
at a different desk. She had been forty-seven and her Polish was excellent and her
understanding of Wrona was approximately as strong as her understanding of anything,
which was to say: technical, thorough, and governed by the belief that translation
meant recovering what the author intended and delivering it intact across the border.
She had treated szarzyzna as a sensory descriptor because that was what her Polish
was equipped to tell her it was.

Now she was sixty-four, and her Polish was arguably less sharp - she read less, spoke
it only with her sister in Poznan, twice a month - and her understanding of Wrona
had changed in a way she could locate but not name. She had been to Krakow three
more times since the translation. She had sat in kitchens. She had watched that
light, not as research but because she was there in November and it was the light
there was. And at some point between then and now szarzyzna had stopped being a word
she looked up and started being a word she felt, and the feeling was not pale gray
light lying across a table but something closer to: here again, the afternoon going,
and it was always going, and nothing is wrong.

She picked up the pen.

*The familiar gray across the table like an extra cloth.*

She looked at it. "Familiar" was reaching toward the recognition rather than the
description. But it was waving a flag - familiar to whom? The narrator? Basia? The
reader? In the Polish, szarzyzna was not claimed by anyone. It was a condition. No
one was calling it familiar because no one needed to. The familiarity was in the word
itself, in the fact that Polish has a single noun for this exact quality of grayness
and everyone who speaks the language knows it the way everyone who lives somewhere
cold knows what the air smells like before snow.

Marta crossed out "familiar."

She was left, again, with the same problem she'd had since ten o'clock. Every
English word she tried either described the light (and missed the recognition) or
named the recognition (and broke the neutrality). The Polish did both at once because
szarzyzna was built out of a lifetime of afternoons, and a word built out of a
lifetime can sit in a sentence without explaining itself.

Her English was not worse than her Polish. It was a different instrument. A language
she had lived in for forty years - longer than she had lived in Polish, if you
counted from the immigration at twenty-two. She thought in English, dreamed in it,
complained in it. But she had not accumulated szarzyzna in English because the
climate was wrong and the kitchens were wrong and the light, even on the grayest
November day in Connecticut, came through differently. The air was drier. The windows
were bigger. The radiators didn't click.

It was possible that the passage was untranslatable. Marta had said this many times
about other passages and been wrong every time - translators who invoke
untranslatability are usually confessing that they haven't thought hard enough. But
szarzyzna resisted her in a way that felt structural rather than verbal. The problem
wasn't that she lacked a word. The problem was that the word's meaning lived in an
experience, and the experience couldn't be carried across in any number of words.

Seventeen years ago she hadn't seen this. She had translated szarzyzna as "pale gray
light" and moved to the next sentence and finished the chapter by the end of the
week and the whole book by March. The translation was good. It was still good. Three
other translators had cited it favorably. Nobody had flagged the passage. The "pale
gray light" worked fine in English because it was competent and appropriate and it
sat in the sentence without calling attention to itself, which is what a translation
is supposed to do.

The question that had brought her back to the desk wasn't whether the original
translation was wrong. It was why it no longer sounded, to her, like the book she
was reading when she opened the Polish.

She looked at both texts side by side. The English, in the Dalkey Archive edition
with the blue cover, open to page 141. The Polish, in the Wydawnictwo Literackie
third printing, open to page 198. The same scene. The same woman in the same kitchen
in the same November light. She read the Polish sentence and felt the gray. She read
the English sentence and saw the gray. These were not the same.

The pen was in her hand and she wrote nothing. Outside, the hedgerow shadow had
reached the foundation. The desk was dimmer than it had been when she started, in
that gradual way that is only visible in the moment you notice you've been squinting.

She closed the Polish text first, then the English. She would not revise the
passage. Not because it couldn't be improved but because the improvement would be a
different translation, made by a different reader, and the book already had a
translation made by the reader she had been, and that translation was as honest as
this one would be, which is to say: partial, in both senses.

The desk light was almost gone. She sat in it.
