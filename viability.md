# Viability

Ruth pulled the accession file before she pulled the seeds. Old habit. The
folder was manila, soft at the edges from handling, and inside it the forms
went back to 1974 when Davor Mitic had hand-printed the collection notes in
block capitals. *Triticum aestivum.* Landrace. Collected from three farms in
the Kolubara valley, elevation 140-180 meters, September 1974. Initial seed
count: 847.

She knew the number without looking. She knew all the numbers for the
collections that mattered, the way you know a phone number you've dialed too
many times.

The viability record ran down the right side of the folder in her own
handwriting, a column of dates and percentages that told the story the way a
doctor's chart tells it -- accurate and insufficient.

1984: 94%
1994: 91%
2004: 88%
2014: 82%

Each entry meant fifty seeds removed from the packet, placed on moistened
blotter paper in a germination chamber at 20 degrees, and watched for fourteen
days. Swollen radicles pushing through the seed coat. Or not. She counted the
ones that pushed through and divided by fifty and wrote the number down and the
fifty seeds, whether they germinated or not, never went back in the packet.
You could not put them back. Once you moved a seed from storage temperature to
germination temperature, the metabolic clock restarted and the seed was no
longer archival material. It was either a plant or waste.

847 minus 200 tested over four decades. Minus another 40 for a regeneration
trial in 1998 that produced enough seed to justify not regenerating the full
lot. Minus a few for a characterization study in 2007 that needed leaf tissue
and grew twelve plants to heading. She could account for every subtraction.

Remaining: 341.

The 2014 result sat at 82%. Institute protocol said regenerate at 85%, and 82%
was below threshold, so the correct action was to grow out a regeneration plot:
plant 100 seeds, harvest the next generation, store the new seed, retire the
old. The new lot would test at 98% and the accession would be secured for
another fifty years.

She had not regenerated.

It was not an oversight. She had written the justification memo herself,
citing the small population size and the genetic risk of a bottleneck
regeneration from only 100 individuals, which was true. She had recommended
waiting for the next scheduled test to confirm the downward trend before
committing, which was reasonable. Paul had signed off because Paul signed off
on everything Ruth recommended, because Ruth had been right about these things
for twenty-six years.

The next scheduled test was now. September 2024. Ten years exactly.

She opened the cold room with her badge and walked between the shelving units
to rack 14, bay 3. The packet was a standard foil laminate, heat-sealed,
labeled in permanent marker with the accession number and Davor's initials. It
weighed almost nothing. Three hundred and forty-one seeds of a wheat that had
grown in a valley that was now under forty meters of reservoir water.

She carried it to the germination lab and set it on the bench and did not open
it.

Fifty seeds for the test. If viability held at 82%, which was optimistic,
forty-one would germinate and nine would not, and the packet would go back
with 291 seeds inside. If viability had dropped further -- and viability
always dropped, entropy was not a negotiation -- maybe thirty-five would
germinate, and the percentage would trigger an immediate regeneration, and she
would plant 100 of the remaining 291, and the regeneration plot would grow a
generation of wheat that had never known the Kolubara valley, that would know
only the institute's research field with its heavy clay and managed irrigation,
and the harvested seed would be genetically Kolubara wheat in the way that a
recording of a voice is genetically someone's voice.

She was being sentimental. She knew that. The whole point of a gene bank was
that genetic material survived the loss of its context. A seed did not need to
remember where it had been to be what it was. The DNA was the DNA regardless.
Every geneticist she worked with would say so. The seed carried the
instructions; the valley was an anecdote.

But Ruth had been testing seeds for thirty years and she did not believe this
fully. She had seen accessions from vanished farms germinate with a vigor that
the regenerated lots never matched, as if the original collection carried some
advantage that a single regeneration cycle in controlled conditions washed out.
She could not prove it. It was not in the data, or it was in the data in a way
the analysis was not built to see -- small differences in seedling
architecture, a tendency in the original lots to push the radicle faster, to
emerge a half-day sooner. Anecdotal. Below statistical significance. The kind
of pattern that thirty years of watching seeds teaches you to see and that
fifteen minutes with a p-value test teaches you to distrust.

She had talked about this with Davor once, five years before he retired. He
had nodded. "The seeds remember the soil," he said, and she had not known
whether he meant it literally or whether this was the mysticism of an old
plant collector talking about his life's work. Perhaps both. Perhaps the
distinction did not hold for a man who had walked the Kolubara valley with a
cloth bag, bending to strip ripe heads from standing wheat while the farmers
watched and their children ran between the rows.

She looked at the packet on the bench.

If she ran the test: 50 seeds consumed. Knowledge gained. 291 remaining.

If she ran the test and then regenerated: 100 more consumed. 191 remaining of
the original, plus several thousand new seeds one generation removed from the
valley floor.

If she did nothing: 341 remaining, viability unknown and almost certainly
declining, the collection sliding toward a threshold below which no
regeneration could recover it.

Each option was a form of loss. Testing lost seeds to gain knowledge.
Regeneration lost provenance to gain security. Inaction lost time. There was
no option that preserved everything.

This was not a crisis. It was the ordinary condition of the work. Every
accession in the cold room was on this curve. Seeds declined, and the only
question was how you spent the declining balance -- on knowledge, on renewal,
or on time. Ruth had spent forty years making this decision well, accession
by accession, and the decisions had all been correct, and the collection was
still smaller every year because correctness consumed material too.

She opened the packet.

The seeds were small and hard and amber-colored, slightly elongated, with a
crease down the middle that deepened as they aged. They looked like every
other bread wheat sample in the cold room. Nothing about their appearance said
Kolubara. Nothing said 1974. Nothing said the farms were now underwater and
the farmers' grandchildren worked in Belgrade. Seeds were not sentimental
objects. They were genetic material in a durable casing, and her job was to
keep the material viable.

She counted fifty into a petri dish. She did it by hand, one at a time, the
way she always did. Each seed clicked against the glass.

Then she counted fifty more.

She set the second dish beside the first and looked at them. One hundred
seeds. The test required fifty. She had counted a hundred.

The thing she was considering was not in any protocol. It was not defensible.
It was, in fact, the opposite of defensible: it was spending double the
required sample to get the same information with slightly better statistics, a
justification so thin she could see through it even as she constructed it.

What she actually wanted was to plant the second fifty. Not in the germination
chamber. In soil. Outside. In October, when winter wheat goes in. She wanted
to see what Kolubara wheat looked like standing in a field. She had tested it
for decades and never seen it grow past the two-leaf stage in a plastic tray
under fluorescent light. She had verified its viability a hundred times and
never once verified what it was viable for.

She put twenty-five seeds back in the packet. She sealed it. 316 remaining.

Fifty for the test. Twenty-five for the field. The rest for whoever came
after her, which would not be long now. Paul had already started asking about
her retirement timeline in the way that managers ask when they have already
decided.

She loaded the first fifty onto blotter paper, added water from the wash
bottle, placed the lid, and slid the tray into the germination chamber.
20 degrees, fourteen days. The protocol was the protocol.

The twenty-five for the field she put in her coat pocket.

Outside, the October sun was low and the institute's research plots were
mostly bare, turned under after the summer harvest. Along the south fence
there was a strip of ground that nobody used, too narrow for trial plots, too
visible for neglect. Ruth had walked past it every day for twenty-six years.

She knelt and pressed seeds into the soil with her thumb, one at a time, an
inch deep, six inches apart. Her knees hurt. The clay was cold. Each seed
went in and disappeared, and the soil closed over it, and there was nothing
to see.

She did not know what would come up. Maybe nothing. Maybe germination was all
they had left. Maybe after fifty years at minus twenty degrees the seeds
could begin and not continue, could crack their coats and push a radicle and
then stop, their development arrested at the same stage she had been measuring
for three decades.

Or maybe not. Maybe in the spring there would be a row of wheat along the
south fence, short and dense the way landraces grow, with awns that caught
the wind differently from the cultivars in the trial plots, and she would
walk past it and see what Davor had seen in the valley before the water rose.

She stood up and brushed the clay from her knees. The empty dish clicked in
her pocket against her badge.

291 in the packet. 50 in the chamber. 25 in the ground.

She went back inside to write it down.
