# Depth

Maren opened the vat at seven because the morning was the clearest
window into what the bacteria had done overnight.

The lid came off heavy with condensation. She set it face-down on the
bench so the droplets would not fall back and carry the room's smell
with them. The vat's own smell rose clean and steady -- fermented grain
under something alkaline, a warmth in the nose that was partly
temperature and partly chemistry. No sourness, no edge. She had
maintained this vat for eleven months and it had been right for the
last five.

The flower covered the surface -- a thin metallic film, blue-bronze
where the light found it, that the bacteria rebuilt overnight from the
oxidized indigo at the air boundary. She had learned to read the flower
the way a gardener reads soil. Thickness, color, how fast it reformed
after being broken. This morning it was even and iridescent, the plates
shifting copper to violet as she moved her head. Good.

She pushed the flower aside with a flat paddle and looked at the liquid.
Yellow-green, clear enough to see the paddle six inches down. At the
surface, where exposed liquid met air, a blue line formed in seconds --
indigo dropping out of solution as oxygen reached it. Below that line:
reduced, soluble, ready to enter fiber.

She dipped a pH strip. 10.4. The thermometer read thirty-eight degrees.
Both where they should be for weeks.

The vat was healthy. The vat had been healthy all week. And for three
days, it had not been building depth the way it should.

She had noticed it on Tuesday. A length of linen, eight dips over the
course of a morning, should have reached the dense, near-black blue she
used for a standing order -- the color that looked like the fabric
carried its own shadow. Tuesday's piece came out evenly dyed, fully
blue, no defects. Just a tone lighter than eight dips had produced all
summer. She had run a ninth to compensate. It brought the piece close.
But close was not the same as right, and an extra dip was not a
diagnosis.

Wednesday confirmed it. Same bolt, same vat, same routine -- two
minutes submerged, lift, rest on the rail while the green turned to
blue, dip again. Eight dips. Same shortfall.

She had spent Wednesday afternoon eliminating the obvious. Reduction
level: she tested a spoonful of liquid on a strip of cotton, and the
blue that developed was strong and immediate. Plenty of indigo in
solution. pH: stable since June. Temperature: steady. The organic feed:
same wheat bran, same schedule since spring. The fabric: same bolt,
same scour. Nothing different that she could find.

Thursday she ran two pieces side by side. One with her usual two-minute
dips. One with four minutes in the vat, same rest between. If the vat
was delivering less per dip, longer exposure should compensate.

Both pieces came out at the same depth. Both a tone light.

This was the result that mattered. If the vat had been weak -- low in
dissolved indigo, poorly reduced -- more time in the liquid should have
meant more uptake. It did not. The vat was delivering the same load per
dip it always had. The shortfall was not in the dip.

Friday morning now. She stood at the rail where the fabric hung between
immersions and noticed what she had been standing next to all week.

The east window was open. It had been open since Sunday, when the
workshop stopped releasing the night's cool by midmorning and the
afternoon heat sat in the room through dark. She had propped the
casement wide because the workshop needed air and the vat liked the
warmth. The breeze through the window crossed directly over the rail.

In June, when she had dialed in her rhythm, the workshop was cool in
the mornings. Twenty degrees, still air. The oxidation of a fresh dip
-- the green-to-blue shift as leuco-indigo took on oxygen -- needed
twelve to fifteen minutes to stabilize. She rested each piece for
twenty minutes. Her rhythm gave each layer time to form before the next
immersion.

The window had been closed in June.

She went to the bench and cut two strips of linen from the same section
of the bolt. Dipped both, pulled both out dripping green, and hung one
on the rail under the window. The other she carried to the back wall,
where the air was still and cool, and hung it from a nail.

The strip on the rail turned blue in eight minutes. The color came in
from the edges and swept toward the center as the moving air touched
one face then the other. Fast, even, done.

The strip on the back wall took fourteen minutes. The blue started at
the top where warmth rose from the floor and worked downward, slower,
the way June's pieces used to turn.

She dipped both strips a second time and hung them in the same places.
This time she did not watch the color. She watched the liquid.

The rail strip shed the vat liquid quickly. It ran off the surface in
sheets. The first layer, fast-set in the warm breeze, had closed tight
-- the oxidized indigo packed dense against the fiber, a smooth face
that the liquid slid over rather than soaking into. The fabric looked
blue and ready.

The back-wall strip held the liquid. The vat's yellow-green clung to
the surface, darkened, took its time finding the fiber. The first layer
was still slightly open -- the oxidized indigo had formed slowly enough
to leave room the liquid could reach. More contact, more of the next
deposit getting in before the surface closed.

Her rhythm had been a measurement she did not know she was making.
Twenty minutes between dips, built in June's cool mornings, tested
against results until it felt right and stopped being a decision. The
timing became part of her hands the way the paddle was part of her arm.
She moved the fabric to the rail, waited until it looked ready, dipped
again. In June, "looked ready" and "was ready" happened at the same
time, and her rhythm settled around them like water around a stone. In
August, "looked ready" arrived six minutes before "was ready." The
color said go. The surface was not yet open. Nothing in the appearance
told her the difference. She had been watching the color. The color was
not the problem.

She left the east window open. The vat needed the warmth and the
workshop needed the air. She unhooked the drying rail from its brackets
and carried it to the back wall, where the light was worse and the air
sat thick and still. She would dye Friday's order there and check the
depth against the standing sample. If it came back, the diagnosis held.
If it did not, there was something else, and these pieces would show
her which direction to look.

She went back to the vat and prepared the first length of linen,
smoothing it through her hands to find any knots in the weave. The
flower had already closed over the spot where she had tested the
liquid, the metallic film rebuilding itself as fast as it always had.
The vat did not know anything had changed. The vat had not been the
problem.
