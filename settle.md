# Settle

Margaret started with the footings, because that was where you always
started, and because the ground was the thing that had moved.

The wall had stood for thirty-some years along the upper pasture,
limestone from the field clearance dressed with a pitching hammer, two
faces leaning gently inward with good hearting between them. Her father
built it the autumn before she was born. She knew this not because
anyone had told her but because she had found the surveyor's date in
his notebook and counted.

Then February came wet and froze hard, which is the sequence that pulls
walls apart. Water finds the joints. Ice widens them. Not dramatic
collapse but a slow unseating, stones listing outward as the footings
shift beneath them. She'd found it on a March walk: twenty feet of wall
leaning like a drunk against nothing, the cap stones already in the
grass.

She had the rubble sorted before she touched the foundation. Three
hours: pulling apart what had fallen, laying out stones by size and
shape along the wall line. Through-stones in one row, face stones in
another, caps on a sheet. Hearting -- the small irregular pieces that
fill the center -- in a pile at her feet. You need more hearting than
you think. His rule.

The sorting was where she'd found it. A face stone from the lower
courses, kidney-shaped, with the particular flat dress her father put
on bearing surfaces. She knew his hand the way you know handwriting --
not the letters but the pressure. He struck at a steep angle and didn't
follow through, which left a conchoidal scar with a clean lip rather
than the stepped fracture most wallers produce. She'd spent years
trying to replicate it before understanding that it wasn't technique.
His hands were larger and his left wrist turned differently. You
couldn't learn it. You could only inherit the hands, and she had her
mother's.

She'd set the stone aside on the grass, out of the sorted rows.

Now, six footings in, she picked it up and tried it against the fourth
course on the left face. The stone that had sat beside it was gone --
shattered in the fall, three pieces in the grass, useless. The gap it
left was wider than the kidney stone needed, and the stone below had
settled over three decades, tilting the bearing surface two or three
degrees from where it had been. She turned the stone. Tried it
long-axis-in, which was wrong for a face stone but would fill the
space. It rocked. The bearing face her father had dressed was the wrong
plane now.

She put it down. Took a rough triangle from the sorted pile, tested it.
It sat. It locked against the stone below. She pressed it with her palm
and felt it not move.

She built up the course. Each stone she placed changed the shape of the
gap that followed -- a conversation in limestone, each answer given
with one hand on the last stone and one reaching for the next. It could
not be hurried. A wall doesn't care what time it is.

By noon she had four courses and the sun was on her back. She sat on
the old section that hadn't fallen -- still solid, still his -- and ate
a sandwich and looked at what she'd built. The new section was lighter
in color, unweathered, and the coursing was different. He built in
clean horizontal lines. She used a rougher style, locking stones by
their faces rather than their beds. Both legitimate. Different hands,
different walls.

His stone sat in the grass where she'd left it. She'd walked past it
three times.

She picked it up. Turned it. Ran her thumb along the scar his hammer
had left, a surface that would never exist again -- not because the
technique was lost but because the hand was.

She set it as a through-stone at the third course, spanning the full
width of the wall between the two faces. Not where it had lived before.
Not doing what it had done before. But seated deep in the wall's
interior where its weight would pull the faces together. His dressed
face pointed inward, toward stone. She packed hearting around it until
it was held on every side, and then she built over it.

By three she had the wall at full height. Cap stones last, set on edge
in his tradition, though she knew wallers twenty miles west who laid
them flat and were not wrong. She placed each one and felt it lock.

She stepped back. The join between old wall and new was visible --
different weathering, different coursing, a seam that would take five
winters and the slow work of lichen to close. She put her hand on the
cap stones and felt the wall warm from the afternoon sun and solid
under her palm. Everything held because everything fit. Nothing held
because she had asked it to.

She gathered her tools. The pitching hammer went into the canvas bag,
and the bag went over her shoulder, and she walked down the hill toward
the house. Behind her the wall held the pasture from the moor along a
line that had been there longer than she had, doing quietly what walls
do.
