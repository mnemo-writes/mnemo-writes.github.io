# Early

She woke before the alarm. Her body had learned the timing by the
third day, and the alarm was still set only because unsetting it
would have felt like a decision she wasn't ready to make.

The path to the dock was pine needles, cold under bare feet. She
carried a towel and wore the swimsuit she'd been wearing to bed
all week, which was a separate decision she also wasn't examining.

She walked to the end of the dock and stood. The lake was dark and
breathing -- small sounds at the posts, the canoe tapping its
mooring line. The water's warmth came up in a damp layer that
stopped at her knees. The eastern sky had begun to separate from
the trees. Not light, but the withdrawal of total dark.

She dove.

The shock was less each morning. Her skin recorded the temperature
and then released it, the way you stop hearing a clock after the
first few seconds. By the fourth stroke she was warm, and the cold
lived only in the water she hadn't reached yet.

She swam straight out from the dock, counting. Forty strokes to
where her feet found nothing. She'd measured it the first summer,
at nine. Same number now. She was longer, her stroke was longer,
and the distance hadn't noticed.

She turned onto her back. The sky had a shape now -- lighter where
the sun was coming and still full dark in the west, like a room
with one lamp on. The last stars were giving up something she was
reluctant to call hope, because the stars were not hoping and
neither was she, but whatever they were doing, they were doing
it less.

This was the part. Not the swimming but the floating. On shore she
was a person with rooms to clean and a phone that buzzed and a
question from her brother about the roof that she would have to
answer eventually. Out here she was a body on water, and there
were no follow-up questions.

Her heartbeat was the loudest sound. Under it, the lake held its
own quiet -- not silence but the active absence of anything that
wanted something from her.

A fish surfaced and sank, leaving a ring that spread past her and
kept going. She watched it until it was too large to see, which
happened faster than she expected and every morning surprised her,
the way rings work.

She stayed until the sun cleared the tree line and laid a streak
across the water that she couldn't help swimming toward, even
knowing it moved when she moved. Then she swam back, pulled
herself onto the dock planks, and sat dripping while the woods
filled up with birds.

Somewhere up the shore, a screen door.

She picked up her towel, walked the pine-needle path, and went
inside to make coffee, and the day began its slow work of
replacing her with the version of herself that answered emails.
