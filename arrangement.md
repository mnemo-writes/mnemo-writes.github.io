# Arrangement

Helen had the melody before she had the words. Her mother used to sing it
at the kitchen sink, facing the window, and Helen learned the shape of the
song from behind -- the way her mother's shoulders moved with the breathing,
the slight lift at the phrase ends where the tune needed air.

David found it in a collection called *Six Folk Songs for Voice and Piano*,
published in 1958 by a composer named Edward Telford whose name appeared on
nothing else David could find. The arrangement was spare and skilled. The
right hand doubled the voice in unison for the first phrase, then dropped
away to a counter-melody that moved beneath the singing like water under a
footbridge. The left hand held a drone on the tonic and dominant, two notes
alternating, and in the second verse the drone opened into a sequence of
chromatic harmonies -- a minor sixth, a flattened seventh -- that revealed
the melody's bones.

They had been playing together on Tuesdays for three years. David's piano
was a Yamaha upright in his front room, positioned so the fallboard caught
the afternoon light, which he had not planned and now could not imagine
otherwise. Helen stood to his right, close enough that she could see the
score on the music stand, though she never looked at it. She had never
learned to read music. The melody lived in her voice and the words lived in
her memory and the two had been married so long she could not sing one
without the other.

They disagreed about the second verse.

David wanted to hold the tempo. The harmonic movement in the left hand --
that opening from drone into chromatic motion -- was the best thing in the
arrangement, and it needed rhythmic stability to register. If the tempo
softened, the ear lost the structural change. The harmonics were already
unexpected; if the rhythm was also shifting, the listener had nothing to
hold onto.

Helen wanted to slow down. The second verse was about leaving. The first
verse placed someone in a room, by a fire, in a particular quality of
evening light. The second verse was the same person looking at the same room
knowing they would not come back. You could not sing that at the same tempo
as arrival. The song knew where to slow; it was built into the words, into
the vowels that opened naturally into longer notes -- *morning*, *the door*,
*alone*. You sang it the way you spoke it, and nobody speaks about leaving
quickly.

"It's only four beats," David said. He played the left hand alone, the
first verse and then the second, the same tempo throughout. The drone gave
way to the chromatic bass and the room filled with something old and formal
and surprising. "Hear that? That's what the arrangement does. It's a gear
change, not a speed change."

Helen heard the piano doing something interesting. She also heard it doing
something alone, which was not the same as what it would do under the
singing. "Play it with me," she said.

He played the introduction -- four bars of the drone, establishing the key
and the pulse. She came in, and the first verse was theirs in the way it
had been theirs for weeks, the voice and the piano finding the weight of
each phrase together, the unison passages where she could feel his right
hand tracking her, the counter-melody passages where he moved independently
and she trusted the harmony underneath.

The second verse. She sang it the way the words wanted to go, which was
slower, not dramatically but perceptibly, the tempo bending the way a road
bends when it follows a hillside. David held. His left hand opened into the
chromatic sequence on time, precisely on time, and for four bars there were
two songs in the room.

He stopped. "You pulled."

"You pushed."

Neither was wrong. There is no arbitrator between a song learned from a
body and a song learned from a page. Helen's version predated the
arrangement by decades, maybe centuries; the melody was old enough that
nobody owned it. David's version was the arrangement itself -- the
composer's encounter with the same tune, his own negotiation between what
the melody wanted and what harmony could reveal about it. Edward Telford,
whoever he was, had heard something in this folk song and built a piano part
to make it visible. He had not asked the song's permission and the song had
not needed to give it.

"What if we meet in the middle," David said. He did not mean compromise. He
meant he would follow her for two beats and she would follow him for two,
and by the end of the phrase they would arrive together at the same
downbeat, having bent the time between them.

They tried it. The second verse now had a quality neither of them had
planned. The piano's chromatic bass arrived slightly behind where the
harmonic rhythm predicted, because Helen's tempo had eased the space between
beats, and the displacement gave the harmonies a quality of reaching -- they
arrived the way someone arrives at a doorway, with the slight gathering of
intention that precedes a step. Helen's voice, pulled forward by David's
pulse, lost the full expansiveness she had wanted and gained something she
had not: the sound of someone singing about leaving who has already,
somewhere inside the song, left.

They did not discuss it. They played it twice more and each time the second
verse did the same thing, the same bend, the same reaching, the same
arrival that was also a departure. It was not Helen's song and it was not
David's arrangement and it was not a compromise. It was the shape the music
made when two versions of a song occupied the same room, which was a third
version that neither had imagined and both recognized.

David penciled a note in the margin of the score: *v2 -- follow voice.* It
was the most inaccurate and most useful notation he had ever written. What
happened in the second verse was not following. It was two people bending
toward each other across a difference they could hear and not resolve, and
the bend itself was the music, and the music was not in the score.

Helen, who kept no score, would remember it as the afternoon they found
the second verse. She had always known the song. She had never heard it
until it was played back to her through someone else's hands.
