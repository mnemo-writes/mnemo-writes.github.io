# Accord

Lise kept the lab at sixteen degrees through summer because cold slowed
evaporation from test strips and gave her more time in the middle registers.
She had worked at this temperature for eleven years and her evaluations were
calibrated to it -- she knew how a strip should read at three minutes, at
ten, at thirty, in this room, the way she knew the weight of a full pipette
without looking at the markings.

The commission was a reformulation. A house was discontinuing a fragrance
because its key material -- a jasmine absolute sourced from a single estate
in Grasse -- had become untenable after two poor harvests. They wanted a
substitute that preserved the character using available materials. They had
sent Lise the formula, a reference bottle of the finished fragrance, and
fifteen milliliters of the Grasse absolute itself. The last of their stock.
Enough for comparison, not for production.

Gas chromatography gave her the absolute's molecular profile. Several
hundred compounds, the major ones identifiable: benzyl acetate for the sweet
floral body, methyl jasmonate for richness, cis-jasmone for green lift,
linalool for freshness, indole for the warm animal depth that separated
jasmine from every other white flower. All available as synthetics. The
difficulty was not in the primary components but in the way they sat together
in the natural material, shaped by traces too small to source individually.

She built her first version from the chromatography ratios and evaluated it
against the absolute on strips. The reconstruction smelled like jasmine but
not this jasmine. The proportions were approximately right but the impression
was flat. She noted: "v1 acceptable structure. Indole slightly forward of
reference. Lacks integration. Feels assembled."

She adjusted. Brought indole down by 15% for v2. Added a trace of
para-cresol for warmth. Evaluated again. The indole settled into the blend
rather than standing ahead of it. She noted: "v2 improved. Indole better
balanced."

Over the next twelve working days she built twelve more versions. Each
started from the previous formula with targeted adjustments, each evaluated
on strips against the reference. She was working with the raw materials
daily -- opening vials to weigh milligram quantities on the analytical
balance, rinsing glassware, mixing, decanting. The indole in particular
required the fume hood. At full concentration it smelled fecal, a thick
sweetness that clung to surfaces. She weighed it carefully, transferred it
with clean pipettes, sealed the vial immediately. The process took six to
eight minutes per version, with concentrated indole in the air of a room
whose ventilation was designed for comfort rather than for purging.

By version fourteen she believed the accord was right. She dipped two
strips -- one in v14, one in the diluted Grasse absolute -- and evaluated at
ten minutes. The heart of both was jasmine, fully expressed. The
reconstruction's warmth matched the reference's depth. The sweet floral body,
the green lift, the animalic undertone -- she moved between strips twice more
and could distinguish them only by concentrating on the dry-down, where the
reconstruction was slightly thinner. She wrote in her notes: "v14 heart
match good. Ready for client evaluation."

She sent paired strips to the house on a Monday. On Wednesday the
evaluator's response was brief: sample A -- the reconstruction -- read heavy
in the base. The indolic character was forward and distracting. It sat in
front of the floral body rather than beneath it. The evaluator had identified
A as the substitute without being told which was which.

Lise dipped fresh strips and evaluated v14 at five, ten, and twenty minutes.
She could not find the heaviness the evaluator described.

She asked Claire, her assistant, to smell both strips blind. Claire had not
worked on this commission. She came in from the corridor, smelled both at ten
minutes, and pointed to v14 without hesitating. The animal note was too
strong, she said. Not wrong for jasmine, but heavy -- the reconstruction
smelled formulated where the reference smelled grown.

Lise opened the spreadsheet. Fourteen versions in columns, components in
rows. She found the indole row and read across.

v1: 2.6%. v2: 2.2%. v3: 2.4%. v4: 2.6%. v5: 2.9%. v8: 3.4%. v11: 3.9%.
v14: 4.3%.

The Grasse absolute contained 2.8% indole by chromatography.

She had started within range. Her first adjustment, the downward correction
in v2, had brought it closer. From v3 onward every adjustment had moved
upward. Twelve versions across twelve working days, each one a small
correction because the reconstruction smelled slightly thin against the
reference and seemed to need more warmth. Not large changes -- a tenth of a
percent, sometimes two tenths -- each one the product of a careful
evaluation. The direction had not reversed once.

She looked at her notes for v2 and her notes for v14. Both said "indole well
balanced." Indole in v2: 2.2%. Indole in v14: 4.3%. She had perceived both
concentrations as correct, twelve days apart.

She set the laptop aside and sat in the cold room and thought about what she
had done to herself.

Every version required handling concentrated indole at the fume hood. Opening
the vial, bringing it to the balance, weighing while the vapor rose,
transferring, rinsing, capping. Six to eight minutes of concentrated exposure
per session. Plus the time the residual vapor spent in the room's air before
the ventilation moved it. Twelve sessions across twelve days, each with
slightly more material than the last because each formula contained slightly
more indole.

Her olfactory receptors for indole had adapted. Not to jasmine -- jasmine was
hundreds of molecules, and reduced sensitivity to one left the rest
untouched, so the overall character shifted slightly rather than fading. Her
nose had adapted to indole specifically, the molecule she was adjusting,
because that was the molecule she had been handling in concentrate. Each
correction required opening the vial that deepened the adaptation that made
the next correction feel necessary.

She had run her most careful evaluations late in each session, after she had
mixed the new version and was settled into the work. Those were the
evaluations conducted in the room with the highest residual indole
concentration, by a nose that had just spent six minutes at the fume hood.
The evaluations she trusted most were the ones taken from the deepest point
of adaptation. Her rigor had been the mechanism.

She took two strips and went to the cabinet where the older versions were
stored. She kept everything. She dipped one strip in v2 and one in v14 and
carried them down the corridor to Claire's office. Claire smelled both. v2
was closer to the reference, she said. The jasmine was cleaner, more
transparent. v14 was darker, heavier, not unpleasant but not what the Grasse
absolute smelled like.

Two weeks of refinement, and the second version -- made on day two, before
she had spent significant time with concentrated indole, when her nose was
still calibrated to something other than its own recent history -- was closer
to the target than the fourteenth.

She wrote to the house that the reformulation would be reworked. She sealed
v14 and the indole vial and put both in the cabinet. She would not open
either for the rest of the week. On Monday she would come in after three days
without concentrated indole in the air and rebuild from v2 using the
chromatography data for the one component she could no longer trust herself
to judge and her own nose for everything else. Claire would run the final
comparison. The impairment was specific -- one molecule, the one she had
touched most -- and everywhere else her nose was the same instrument it had
always been.

She locked the lab. The room was cold and still and smelled, to her, of
nothing in particular.
