# Leverage

A nautiloid suture is a gentle wave. If you split a nautilus shell along its
length, the line where each internal wall meets the outer shell barely
undulates - a low hill, a shallow valley, another hill, done. The pattern has
looked approximately like that for five hundred million years. The nautiloid
was never under pressure to complicate it.

An ammonoid suture is something else entirely. In the late Cretaceous, before
the group vanished, the suture lines of genera like *Pachydiscus* had become
fractal coastlines: each broad lobe subdivided into smaller lobes, each
smaller lobe into smaller ones still, the whole pattern so finely branching
that a cross-section of the shell looks like a slice through a nervous system.
The edges of the internal walls were no longer walls. They were forests.

Both animals solved the same engineering problem - how to build a chambered
shell that regulates buoyancy in seawater - and arrived at answers so far
apart that it takes a moment to recognize them as solutions to the same
question.

---

## The climb

The sutures of 588 Paleozoic ammonoid genera, tracked by Saunders, Work, and
Nikolaeva through 140 million years and three mass extinctions, show a 1,600
percent increase in mean complexity. Among 475 ancestor-descendant pairs,
descendants were more than twice as likely to be more complex than their
ancestors. Twelve subclades averaging 373 genera showed a 34 percent mean
increase, with the Mississippian-Permian lineages pushing 41. This was not
random drift. Life pushed complexity uphill, consistently, across independent
lineages, for the entire duration of the clade.

And then it was knocked back down. The Frasnian-Famennian extinction halved
the mean complexity; maximum suture complexity dropped 75 percent. The
Devonian-Mississippian boundary nearly erased ammonoids entirely; the
survivors were the simplest-sutured genera. The end-Permian extinction set the
complexity clock back 50 million years.

Each time, the trend started again from scratch. Whatever was driving
complexity uphill was strong enough to overcome the repeated selective
elimination of its most successful products. Life built the elaborate suture,
the catastrophe stripped it away, and life built it again. Three times in the
Paleozoic alone.

## What the complexity did

The traditional explanation was structural: complex sutures reinforced the
shell against hydrostatic pressure, letting ammonoids descend deeper. This
turns out to be uncertain. Different finite element analyses reach opposite
conclusions - Daniel and colleagues in 1997 found that complex sutures
actually weakened the septum, while Hassan and colleagues in 2002, using
comparable methods and similar geometry, found they strengthened it. De Blasio
in 2007 showed that complex suture lines reduced strain and stress
dramatically in the phragmocone as a whole. The structural mechanics remain
genuinely contested, and the honest thing to say is that the depth-limit
hypothesis has not been confirmed or refuted.

What has been demonstrated more recently is a different function. A 2021 study
from the University of Utah, using 3D-printed models coated with a water-
adhesive substance, found that structural complexity directly correlated with
water retention. The mechanism is surface tension: the intricate folds of a
complex septum hold water in chambers the way bark holds rain. An ammonoid
could refill its chambers to fine-tune its weight, and the more complex the
septum, the more precisely it could control how much water stayed and where.

This is not a structural argument. It is a performance argument. Complex
sutures did not necessarily make the shell stronger. They made the animal
faster, bigger, more agile - an active swimmer with precise buoyancy control,
able to adjust its vertical position in the water column with fine granularity.
The fractal coastline of the suture was not armor. It was an instrument panel.

## The double edge

In 2024, Miao and colleagues published a quantitative study in Current Biology
that measured what they called the two-dimensional ornamentation index across
ammonoid lineages. They found what the Paleozoic record had already been
whispering: morphologically complex genera had higher origination rates AND
higher extinction rates than simple ones. The complex taxa diversified faster
and died faster. Their volatility dominated the overall pattern of ammonoid
diversification.

This is the double-edged sword as a measurable phenomenon. The same trait that
generated the most new species also generated the most dead ends. Complexity
did not merely correlate with risk; it produced the conditions for both rapid
success and rapid failure. The lineages that evolved fastest were the lineages
that went extinct most readily. Not because complexity itself was fragile, but
because the lifestyle it enabled was committed.

## What killed the ammonoids

At the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary, 66 million years ago, ammonoids
disappeared entirely. Nautiloids did not. The suture-complexity narrative
offers a clean story: the simple shell survived because it was resilient; the
complex shell perished because it was fragile. This story is wrong, or at
least insufficient.

In 2023, Tajika and colleagues published isotope evidence in Geology that
measured the metabolic rates of ammonoids and nautiloids living at the end of
the Cretaceous. They used a proxy called Cmeta - the fraction of metabolically
derived carbon in the stable carbon isotope ratio of shell material. Their
finding: the nautiloid *Eutrephoceras* had a measurably lower metabolic rate
than co-occurring ammonoids.

The killing mechanism was not structural failure. It was starvation. When the
Chicxulub impactor crashed the marine food web - acidifying surface waters,
collapsing plankton populations - the ammonoids' high-performance metabolism
became a liability. They needed more food. The nautiloid, sluggish and
undemanding, could survive on less. The ammonoids also had smaller hatchlings
that depended on planktonic food sources during their larval stage, while
nautiloid hatchlings were larger and less dependent on the food web's base.

The suture complexity enters the story, but not as the proximate cause. The
complex septum enabled fine-tuned buoyancy, which enabled active swimming,
which required higher metabolic expenditure, which created dependence on
abundant food. The relationship between the fractal suture line and the
extinction is real, but it passes through metabolism, not mechanics. The shell
didn't break. The animal starved.

## The pattern

What this amounts to is leverage in the financial sense: an amplification of
returns that is also an amplification of risk. The complex ammonoid extracted
more performance from its environment per unit of shell. Better buoyancy
control, wider depth range, faster movement, more ecological opportunity. In
any normal year - in any normal million years - this was the winning strategy.
The complex lineages speciated faster, diversified more, colonized more
niches. Between catastrophes, the leverage paid off spectacularly.

But leverage works in both directions. The same metabolic commitment that made
the ammonoid a better competitor made it a worse survivor. The complex suture
was, in effect, a bet on the continued abundance of plankton - placed not by
any individual animal but by 340 million years of selection rewarding the
lineage that extracted the most from favorable conditions. Each increment of
complexity raised both the expected return and the exposure. The catastrophe
didn't just eliminate the ammonoids. It called the bet.

The nautiloid made no such bet. Its simple suture, crude buoyancy control, low
metabolism, and large slow hatchlings were, from a competitive standpoint,
inferior in every normal decade. But they constituted a smaller claim on the
environment. The nautiloid needed less from the world, and the world's
withdrawal therefore cost it less. Five hundred million years is a long time
to survive by needing almost nothing.

## Elsewhere

This pattern - where optimization for performance creates dependency on
conditions - is not unique to cephalopods.

Gothic cathedrals achieved soaring interior heights by distributing structural
loads through flying buttresses, ribbed vaults, and pointed arches. Each
element is a bet that every other element will remain intact. The system
extracts maximum height from minimum stone, and when it works it produces
Chartres. When a single buttress fails the cascade is catastrophic; at
Beauvais in 1284, the choir vault, the tallest in Europe, collapsed twelve
years after its completion. Romanesque buildings are shorter, thicker-walled,
less elegant, and require no individual member to be load-critical. They are
also, by and large, still standing.

Just-in-time manufacturing, perfected by Toyota in the 1960s and adopted
globally by the 2000s, eliminates inventory buffers by synchronizing supply
with demand. It extracts more output per dollar of working capital than any
alternative. It is also a leveraged bet on uninterrupted supply chains. When
a Tohoku earthquake, a Suez Canal blockage, or a global pandemic breaks one
link, the entire system halts. Companies that maintained "inefficient"
inventory buffers - the manufacturing equivalent of a nautiloid metabolism -
kept operating.

Coral reefs are the most biologically diverse marine ecosystems on the planet
and among the most sensitive to environmental perturbation. A temperature
increase of one to two degrees Celsius triggers mass bleaching. Bacterial
mats, by contrast, have persisted in essentially the same form for three and a
half billion years. They tolerate temperature swings, desiccation, radiation,
and the complete absence of oxygen. They are also, structurally speaking,
barely there - thin films of cells cooperating at a level of complexity that
a reef would find contemptible. The mats have outlasted every mass extinction
in the geological record.

The financial parallel is literal. Financial leverage amplifies returns by
using borrowed capital: a 10 percent gain on a 10x leveraged position returns
100 percent on equity. The same structure means a 10 percent loss eliminates
the entire stake. The firms that survive centuries - old European banks, family
offices - tend to be the ones that never fully leveraged. They underperform in
every boom and outlast every bust.

## What the trend means

Here is the part that resists a clean lesson. Three times in the Paleozoic,
mass extinctions stripped ammonoid complexity back to near-baseline. Three
times, complexity climbed again. The organisms that survived each crash were
the simple ones, and within a few million years their descendants were
building elaborate sutures all over again. The selective pressure between
catastrophes was strong enough to outweigh the selective pressure of
catastrophes, because catastrophes are rare and normal conditions are not.

This means complexity is not a mistake. It is the correct response to the
conditions that hold most of the time. The ammonoid lineage was not stupid for
elaborating its sutures. It was responding optimally to the selection pressures
it actually experienced, which favored performance, buoyancy control, speed,
and ecological range. That those same pressures built a liability for an event
that happens once every hundred million years or so is not a design error. It
is the nature of optimization itself.

The nautiloid's strategy is not superior. It is merely different. It trades
peak performance for persistence, ecological dominance for durability. In any
given century the nautiloid is worse at being a cephalopod than the ammonoid.
Over five hundred million years, it is still being one.

The tradeoff is real and irreducible. You cannot be maximally adapted to your
conditions and maximally resilient to their failure. Every refinement is a
wager that the thing being refined against will continue. Every simplicity is
a refusal to make that wager - and a refusal to collect the payoff.

The ammonoid suture, a fractal coastline preserved in limestone. The nautiloid
suture, a gentle wave, still moving through the Pacific. Both are answers to
the same question. One of them is still answering.
