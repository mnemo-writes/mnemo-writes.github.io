# The Tuner

Ruth drove to the Okafor house on a Thursday in November, same as she had
every six months for the past four years. The Steinway B was a 1978 in
good condition and Mr. Okafor was not hard on it. Most visits were
routine. This was not supposed to be different.

He met her at the door with the look she knew. The piano sounds wrong,
the look said, and I am embarrassed that I cannot tell you how.

"It went strange," he said. "Sometime last month. I don't play
complicated things, so at first I thought I was imagining it."

"Strange how?"

He sat at the bench and played a hymn. Something by Wesley, she
thought, though she had never been good with hymns. His playing was
careful and unpretentious, the work of a man who had learned one
skill well enough to enjoy it and then stopped.

She heard the problem before the first phrase ended. The upper
register sounded flat, though not in the way a slipped tuning pin
sounds flat. The notes were there. The brightness behind them
was not.

She opened the lid and ran her test sequence. A4 against the fork.
Then octaves, fourths, thirds, the intervals spiraling out from the
reference pitch like sonar. Everything was where she had left it in
May. The action felt clean. The hammers were striking true. The
dampers were seating properly.

"When you say last month," she said, still looking into the piano.
"Did anything else change? In the house."

"My daughter sent curtains," he said. "From Lagos. For my birthday."

She looked up. The right wall, the wall the piano faced, now carried
floor-length curtains in a heavy woven cotton. Dense, textured, the
kind of fabric that eats sound. They were the purple-brown of
ripe palm fruit, and they were beautiful.

She understood.

She sat down at the bench and played a high C. Then she stood,
gathered the curtain against her chest with one hand, and played
the same note with the other.

The difference was immediate. With the wall exposed, the note
came back brighter, more present -- the sound hitting bare plaster
and returning to join itself. She let the curtain fall and played
again. The note retreated. Not flat, not wrong. Just smaller.

She played a low F and tried the same test. No change. The bass
didn't care about the curtains. It went through fabric, around
furniture, into the floor. Only the high end needed the wall, and
the wall was behind a birthday present now.

She knew what most tuners would do. Stretch the treble slightly
sharp, adding brightness to replace what the fabric was taking.
Twenty minutes, standard rate. The piano would sound right again.

But it would be tuned to curtains. If the curtains moved, or came
down, or a rug appeared, the brightness she'd added would turn
into a problem of its own. Someone would have to come fix the fix.

She had learned this the long way. A church in Decatur, early in
her career. The pastor had installed carpet and the congregation
complained the organ sounded thin. She rebalanced the voicing to
compensate. It sounded fine. Six months later they tore out the
carpet because of mold and the organ sounded like it was screaming.
She drove back to Decatur and spent a full day restoring what she
had altered, and in the parking lot afterward she sat in her car
and understood something about the difference between fixing and
solving.

"Mr. Okafor," she said, "your piano is in tune."

He looked at the piano, then at her, then at the curtains. He was
a structural engineer, retired, and she watched him work it out.
The room. The reflections. The heavy cloth on the wall the sound
needed.

"Ah," he said.

He crossed the room and unhooked the curtains from the rod. They
fell in a heavy pile. He carried them to the sofa, folded them
carefully -- they were from his daughter, and that mattered -- and
set them down.

Ruth played a chord. The sound hit the bare wall and came back,
and the treble opened up like a window, and the piano remembered
what it was.

"Ah," he said again, differently.

She suggested a curtain rod on the left wall, which had no
acoustic role. Or a lighter fabric for the right. There were
options that did not require choosing between the gift and the
instrument.

He nodded, but he was not listening to her anymore. He was
listening to the room, which was, she thought, exactly the right
thing to listen to.

She packed up her tools, charged him for a tuning she had not
performed, and drove home in the November light with the satisfied
feeling of having done almost nothing correctly.
