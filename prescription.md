# Prescription

The wind sock on the ridge hangs limp, then lifts, then drops. She
watches it from the truck with a thermos balanced on the dash and the
prescription in her lap, a document she wrote six months ago for a day
that might be today.

Relative humidity: 25 to 45 percent. Wind speed: 3 to 12 miles per
hour, southwest. Temperature: above 50 degrees Fahrenheit. One-hour
fuel moisture: 4 to 8 percent. Transport wind: below 20 knots. Mixing
height: above 1,500 feet. The window is narrow because the window is
supposed to be narrow. Too dry and the fire crowns. Too wet and it
won't carry. Too much wind and it jumps the line. Too little and the
smoke lies down in the drainage and doesn't lift.

She wrote these numbers at a desk in January, pulling weather records
from the previous ten years, running fuel models through BehavePlus
until the outputs looked like a fire she wanted. The fire she wanted
was slow and thorough: a backing fire moving into the wind at a walking
pace, dropping the temperature in the litter layer long enough to kill
the sweetgum seedlings and the privet but short enough to leave the
longleaf bark intact. Longleaf pine evolved for this. Its terminal bud
wears a tuft of needles as thick as a shaving brush, and it wraps its
growing tip in a wet sheath that steams rather than ignites. It wants
fire. The understory that chokes it in fire's absence is the injury,
not the flame.

Forty acres. Twelve hundred feet of fire line cut with a dozer two weeks
ago, a brown scar scraped to mineral soil along three sides. The fourth
side is the county road. She walked the line yesterday, checking for
root channels that could carry fire underground and for gaps where a
log had fallen across. Found two. Bucked the logs with a saw, scraped
the gap, packed the raw dirt flat with her boot. The line is as good as
she knows how to make it, which is not the same as good enough. She
has seen fire cross lines wider than this when the conditions said it
shouldn't. The conditions said. The fire didn't read them.

The Kestrel is in her vest pocket. She pulls it out and holds it up and
the little fan spins. Fifty-eight degrees. Relative humidity thirty-one
percent. Wind: four miles per hour out of the west-southwest. She writes
the numbers on the back of the prescription with a grease pencil. They
fit. They have fit since seven this morning, and she has checked five
times because checking is cheaper than being wrong.

The crew is at the staging area by the cattle gate. Eight people. Two
have done this burn before, on this unit, in this fuel type. The other
six have done burns, some of them many burns, but not this one. She
briefed them at seven. Showed them the map. Walked the boundary on
paper, pointing out the snag on the northeast corner -- a dead pine,
still standing, hollowed by beetle kill, which will catch and burn
internally and throw embers -- and the drainage on the south side where
smoke will pool if the wind drops below three. Assigned positions.
Interior ignition: herself and Luis, because Luis is calm and doesn't
speed up. Holding: three on the east flank, two on the south, one
at the snag with a charged hose line. She said: "If it does something
you don't understand, tell me. I'd rather stop the burn than explain
why I didn't."

The spot forecast came in at six. She reads it again. Southwest 5 to 10,
gusting 15. Mixing height 2,200 feet. Transport winds at 3,000 feet,
15 knots. Haines Index 5, moderate. Nothing in it she hasn't already
seen in the sky: high scattered clouds moving southwest to northeast,
steady and unhurried, the kind of cloud motion that means the upper
atmosphere agrees with what's happening at the surface.

She finishes the thermos. Gets out. The air has the dry particular smell
of October in the sandhills -- pine straw and warm sand and something
vegetal underneath, the wiregrass that hasn't flowered in four years
because it hasn't burned in four years. Wiregrass flowers after fire.
Nobody knows exactly why. The signal might be ethylene in the smoke,
or the sudden exposure to light, or the nutrient flush from the ash.
Nobody has isolated it. She has watched it happen twice and still finds
it strange -- a plant that waits for the thing that should kill it and
answers by flowering.

She walks to the truck bed. Two drip torches, red canisters with bent
spouts, filled this morning with the standard mix: three parts diesel
to one part gasoline. The diesel burns slow. The gasoline lights it.
Neither is the fire. The fire is the fuel on the ground and the air
above it and the heat between them. All the drip torch does is introduce
the idea.

Ten-twenty. She keys the radio. "Dispatch, Sandhill Fourteen, requesting
ignition authorization."

The answer takes forty seconds, which is normal and which she uses to
check the Kestrel one more time. Fifty-nine degrees. RH twenty-nine.
Wind five, southwest. The numbers are better than when she checked five
minutes ago. They will stay better for three or four hours, until the
afternoon humidity recovery begins and the window closes from the wet
side. She has time. She does not have time to waste.

"Sandhill Fourteen, you are authorized for ignition. Contact dispatch at
completion or if conditions change."

She lights the drip torch. The wick catches with a small sound, diesel
burning orange at the tip of the bent spout. She tilts it and a drop of
burning fuel falls into the pine straw at the edge of the fire line.

The straw catches. A ring of flame the size of a dinner plate, spreading
outward at the pace of a minute hand.

She walks the line. Every ten feet she tilts the torch and leaves
another drop. Each drop becomes a circle, and the circles expand until
they merge, and the merged line of fire begins to back into the wind.
Slow. A foot a minute, maybe less. The flames are six inches tall,
pale orange in the daylight, nearly invisible against the brown litter.
This is the fire she wrote the prescription for: low, unhurried,
patient with the fuel.

She works the entire eastern line this way, then crosses to the south
and begins again. Luis follows her by two hundred feet, lighting
behind her on the interior, building strips of fire that will burn
toward the backing fire and consume themselves at the meeting. Strip-
head fire: each strip runs with the wind toward the last, covering
more ground than the backing fire alone but limited by the spacing.
Light them too close and they merge into a head fire that moves too
fast. Too far apart and the strips burn out before they meet, leaving
unburned islands. The spacing is a judgment, not a formula, and she
has been making it for eleven years.

The smoke column rises cleanly. Straight up, then tilting northeast
where the transport wind carries it. She watches the column the way a
sailor watches the horizon -- not for what it shows but for the change
in what it shows. A column that leans too hard means the surface wind
is beating the convection. A column that goes vertical and mushrooms
means the fire is generating its own weather. This column is doing
neither. It rises and bends, rises and bends, like a long exhalation
that doesn't end.

Thirty minutes in. A quarter of the unit is black. The holding crew
radios nothing, which is the correct thing to radio.

She stops at the drainage on the south side. Smoke is pooling here as
expected -- flowing downhill like water, collecting in the low ground.
She can see it settling. She doesn't light the drainage. She'll come
back to it last, when the fire on both sides has burned down and the
flanking heat can pull the drainage fire uphill and out. Burning it
now would trap smoke in the hollow for an hour.

This is the part she has tried to explain to people who haven't done it,
and she can't. The planning takes months. The preparation takes weeks.
The ignition takes minutes. And then the fire does what fire does, and
her job is to not interfere with it. Not to chase it, not to push it,
not to panic when a flame front pauses at a patch of bare sand and
looks like it might go out. It will find fuel. It has been finding fuel
for longer than anyone has been managing it.

The hardest moment is always the same. Not the lighting, not the
holding, not the mop-up. It's the twenty minutes after ignition when
the fire is established and moving and she has to let it. She has
set the conditions. The conditions are good. The fire is behaving. And
every instinct says to do something, to adjust, to add, to intervene,
because standing in a burning forest watching fire eat the ground and
choosing not to act feels like negligence from inside and looks like
confidence from outside and is neither. It's discipline, which is the
decision to trust the conditions you set when you were calm over the
impulse you feel when you are present, and it has a physical weight
she can feel in her hands and her jaw.

She stands and watches the fire back through a thicket of sweetgum
saplings. The thin bark splits. The leaves curl and flash. In thirty
seconds the thicket is a stand of black sticks, and the fire has moved
past it into the open longleaf understory where the wiregrass carries
it in a low steady line.

An hour fifteen. Two-thirds of the unit is black. The snag on the
northeast corner is burning internally -- she can see the smoke coming
out of the beetle holes in the trunk, slow and white, which means it's
consuming the punky wood inside. She radios the holder at the snag.
"How's it looking?"

"Burning but standing. No throwback."

"Stay on it. When it falls, it'll fall northeast. Stay south."

"Copy."

She lights the drainage last. The flames catch in the heavy fuel -- leaf
litter and fallen branches that have accumulated in the low ground
for four years -- and the fire burns hotter here than anywhere else
on the unit. She watches it. The flanking heat from the burned
hillsides above draws the smoke upward and out of the hollow, as
planned. The flames in the drainage reach waist height, which is taller
than she wanted and within prescription, and they burn for four minutes
before the heavy fuel is consumed and the flame height drops to inches.

By two o'clock the unit is black. The fire has eaten everything it was
given and stopped at the lines she drew for it. Small smokes rise from
stumps and root channels. The holding crew walks the perimeter, turning
over duff with Pulaskis, feeling for heat with bare hands. She walks
the interior. The ground is warm through her boot soles. The longleaf
pines stand untouched above the char, their crowns green, their thick
bark barely scorched. At the base of one she finds a wiregrass clump
already curling a new blade from the blackened tussock. She doesn't
pick it up. She doesn't need to.

She keys the radio. "Dispatch, Sandhill Fourteen. Burn complete, all
lines held. Commencing mop-up."

She unscrews the drip torch and caps it and puts it in the truck. The
work that remains is unglamorous and takes longer than the burn itself:
three hours of walking, checking, digging out hot spots within fifty
feet of the line. The fire is done, and she will tend what it did until
she's satisfied that every edge of what it did is where she said it
should be.

The wind sock on the ridge lifts and steadies. Southwest, eight miles
per hour. Still in prescription. The fire doesn't care. It's finished.
