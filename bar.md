# Bar

She boarded at the pilot station in the dark, forty minutes before
first light, climbing the rope ladder up the hull of a container ship
that was large enough to have its own weather. The hull was wet and the
rungs were cold and there was a swell running and the ladder swung away
from the steel and back against it in a rhythm she climbed through
without thinking about.

On the bridge wing the master was waiting. Swedish flag, Danish master,
Filipino crew. The master was in his fifties, compact, clean uniform, the
look of a man who had brought this ship into a hundred ports and
preferred to have the paperwork in order before the pilot arrived. He
did. Draft 13.4 meters. LOA 334. Engine response time 15 seconds ahead,
22 astern. Rudder hard-over 28 seconds. Bow thruster. She took the card
and read the numbers she already knew from the pre-arrival report and
gave them back.

"Current state of the bar?"

"Charted controlling depth 15.1 at mean low. You'll have 16.9 with
the tide. Last survey was October 14th." She paused. "There was a
northwest storm on the 28th. Survey boat is scheduled for next week."

He looked at her.

"I crossed yesterday outbound on a bulk carrier. The channel was where
the chart says it is." She did not say: yesterday the channel was
where the chart says it was. Today is a different sentence. She had
learned early that precision in a pilot's language had to be invisible
or it alarmed people.

She took the conn. The helmsman was steady, young, already watching the
compass rather than the window. She told him the headings she'd want and
the order she'd want them in, and he nodded. She brought the ship around
to the approach heading and they moved toward the bar at seven knots,
which was the speed that gave her steerage without carrying too much
momentum if something needed to change.

There was fog on the water but not in the air. A surface fog, sitting
on the river like a poured layer, the tops of the channel markers
standing above it and the bases invisible. She could see the sea
beyond the bar, the long northwest swell rolling in gray and even,
and she could see the fog, and she could see the markers, and she could
not see the water.

She waited. Fog like this lifts when the first light hits it, or it
doesn't. Nothing to do about it either way. The ship moved at seven
knots and the bar approached at seven knots and those were the same
fact.

At 0558 the light reached the fog from the east and the surface turned
from gray to white and then to nothing. She could see the water.

The first thing was the color. The deep channel was a dark green that
swallowed the light down and gave nothing back. The shoals on either
side were a paler green with brown in it, the light going to the
bottom and returning with the color of the sand. The boundary between
them was the edge of the navigable channel, and it was not where the
chart said it was.

She looked at the chart on the screen beside her. The channel ran
straight and centered between the red and green buoys, 200 meters
wide, the 15-meter depth contour a smooth pair of parallel lines.

She looked at the water. The dark water ran between the same buoys
but it did not run centered. It was shifted east, the deep color
pulling away from the western buoys and crowding the eastern ones.
The western buoys stood in pale water. They marked a depth that had
been there in October and was not there now.

She looked further ahead. At the bar crest, where the channel was
shallowest and a ship with 13.4 meters of draft had the least room
to be wrong, a low swell was heaving and not breaking. Not a wave.
A lift. The water humping up over something solid beneath it and
falling back without cresting. That hump was the signature of a
shoal too deep to break waves in this swell but shallow enough to
shape them. It sat in the western half of the charted channel. It
had not been there in October either.

"Come starboard to zero-four-five," she said.

The helmsman repeated it and brought the wheel over. The ship began
its slow pivot, 334 meters of steel changing direction like a
conversation changing subject, not all at once but gradually, the bow
swinging first and the stern following.

The new heading put them 80 meters east of the charted center line.
The eastern buoys would be close aboard to port. She had room. The
dark water was wide enough on this side, the color clean and deep.

The master had been watching the chart display. He looked at the
buoys, then at her.

"We're east of the track."

"Sand has moved," she said. "The deep water is east of center today."

He looked at the water. She knew what he was seeing: water. Green
water, dark water, pale water. A surface that looked the same to him
everywhere and different to her everywhere. He was a good shiphandler
in open sea and he trusted pilots in restricted waters because that
was the system and the system was rational, but he was trusting a
person who was steering his ship away from the charted channel on
the basis of the color of the water.

"The shoal is there." She pointed to the hump, the low swell lifting
ahead and to the west. "See the swell heaving up without breaking?
There's sand under that. Fifteen meters charted but it's shallowed.
How much I don't know. Enough to shape the swell."

He looked where she pointed. She could see him trying to see it,
a man who had spent his career reading instruments and charts and
weather trying to read water. He would not see it the way she saw
it. The way she saw it was the product of 23 years of crossings,
two a day, sometimes three, four thousand transits of a bar that was
never the same bar twice, and what she saw was not information she
could name in the chart's language. She saw tension in the surface
where the swell met resistance. She saw a boundary between two
greens that belonged to two different depths. She saw current lines,
fine wrinkles on the surface where water moving at one speed met
water moving at another, and the lines told her where the channel
walls were, not where the buoys said they were.

"I see it," he said. She did not think he saw it. He saw that she
saw it, which was a different thing and enough.

The bar crest passed beneath them. The depth sounder, which she had
been watching in the corner of her eye the way she watched everything
that gave numbers, read 16.2. Then 15.8. Then 15.6.

She did the arithmetic without doing arithmetic: the sounder read
15.6, the ship drew 13.4, 2.2 meters of water between keel and
sand. On the charted track, through the shoal, a meter less depth.
One point two meters between the keel and the sand, on a ship
moving at seven knots, in a swell that was lifting and dropping
the hull half a meter each cycle. That was not a number she would
have accepted.

The depth sounder climbed. 16.1. 17.4. 19. The bar fell away beneath
them and the river opened ahead, wide and deep and brown, and the
channel markers stood in a patient line leading upriver to the port
twelve miles away.

She brought the ship back to the center line. The rest of the transit
was the charted channel and the charted channel was the real channel
and there was no argument between them.

She would file a Notice to Mariners when she got ashore: shoaling
observed in the western portion of the bar, estimated depth reduction
of one to two meters from the charted 15.1, recommend all deep-draft
vessels favor the eastern side until the next survey confirms the
extent. The notice would go out as text in a system of texts. The
harbor master would read it. The next pilot would read it. The survey
boat, when it arrived next week, would run lines over the bar with a
multibeam sounder and produce a new chart with new contour lines and
the new chart would be correct and present-tense for the duration of
the transit from the survey vessel's printer to the next storm.

The master was on the bridge wing as she prepared to disembark. The
container ship would berth under harbor tugs now, his own crew on the
lines.

"Thank you, pilot."

"Fair winds."

She descended the ladder to the pilot boat, which had been running
alongside in the lee of the hull. The coxswain brought it in close
and she stepped across the gap at the bottom of the ladder, one
foot on the rung, one on the gunwale, the swell lifting both vessels
at almost but not quite the same rate. She let go of the ladder and
was on the boat and the boat pulled away and the container ship
continued upriver.

She sat in the cabin and wrote her notes while the crossing was
still in her body. The bar had moved. The western shoal was new.
She drew a sketch on the back of the pilot card, the kind of drawing
that looked like nothing to anyone who had not been there: a curve
for the bar, an oval for the shoal, an arrow for the deep water, a
number for the depth that mattered. She would type the notice when
she got to the office. The sketch would stay in her pocket.

The pilot boat turned for the station. Another ship was waiting at
the anchorage, a tanker, lighter draft, less concern. She would
board it in an hour. The bar would be the same bar it had been
twenty minutes ago, or it would not. She would look at the water and
the water would tell her which.
