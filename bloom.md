# Bloom

Nils opened the cave at six because the wheels needed turning before
the morning's milk arrived, and because the cave needed checking
before the building above it warmed.

Twelve degrees and ninety-three percent humidity, which he felt before
he read the panel by the door. Cold that sat on the skin without
moving. Air heavy enough that breathing felt like drinking. The smell
arrived a beat after the cold -- the compound signal of a hundred and
forty wheels at various stages of becoming what they would become.
Ammonia, hay, wet stone, and beneath them a sweetness that meant the
surface cultures were working. You could not separate it from the rest,
the way you cannot hear a single instrument in an orchestra warming
up. You knew it by its presence or its absence.

He pulled the door shut behind him and did not let it stay open longer
than the step through required.

The shelves ran floor to ceiling on three walls, spruce planks on
steel brackets. Youngest wheels at the bottom, oldest at the top. The
progression up the shelves was visible even in the low light: white
and chalky near the floor, deepening through ivory to the mottled
orange and pink of mature rinds at the top. A visitor saw cheese
aging on shelves. Nils saw a column of habitats, each shelf its own
climate, each wheel a population at a different stage of succession.

He started at the bottom, where he always started. The newest wheels
were still white, still smelling of fresh curd and brine. He turned
each one a quarter clockwise so that the face that had been pressing
the spruce was now exposed to the air. His hands touched every wheel
as he turned it. Not looking for anything particular. His hands knew
what a wheel at this stage felt like and would register a departure
before his mind named it. Smooth and slightly tacky at one day old.
Sixteen wheels. Nothing wrong.

The second shelf held wheels at one to two weeks, their surfaces
covered in a fine white fuzz that looked like frost. This was the
first bloom -- the initial colonizers that did the work of changing
the rind's chemistry so that what followed could take hold. He washed
these with a cloth dipped in four-percent brine, one pass over each
face and the edge. The wash managed the growth, kept it thin enough
that the surface stayed open for the organisms that needed to come
next. A heavy coat sealed the rind like mulch piled too thick --
protected what was underneath but smothered what was trying to come up
through it.

The cloth was a diagnostic surface. It picked up whatever was on the
rind and presented it at arm's length. He wrung the cloth between
every second wheel, re-dipped, checked the color and the smell of
the fabric. Clean wash, no off-colors. The bloom was even and light.
Second shelf was fine.

Third shelf. Two to three weeks. The transition zone, where the white
should be giving way to color. Pale peach at first, then deeper
salmon, the pigment arriving as the second community established
itself on what the first had built. The handoff was not a
replacement. The initial bloom receded, thinned, became substrate
rather than surface. The next community built on what the first had
changed -- the acidity, the available nutrients, the texture of the
rind itself. Each layer made the conditions for the layer that
followed.

He washed the first three wheels on the third shelf. Good color
development, the orange coming in evenly from the edge toward the
center of each face. Turned them, moved on.

The fourth wheel stopped him.

Not the look. The look was close enough -- slightly less color than
its neighbors, the orange thinner, a section near the edge still
showing white where it should have cleared by now. Unevenness at
twenty days was common. Wheels varied because the milk varied, the
pressing varied, the initial salt varied. A lagging section could
catch up in a day.

The smell stopped him. He held the cloth near the surface and
breathed. The normal signal at this stage was ammonia over something
sweeter -- the earthy funk that made people either love or refuse the
finished cheese. This wheel had that, but quieter than the three he
had just washed. Muted. And underneath the expected smell, something
else. Thinner. Sharper. Not sour, which was a different problem
entirely. This was closer to green wood. Vegetal. Faint enough that
he would have missed it if he had not been comparing it, in immediate
memory, with three wheels that smelled right.

He set the wheel down and moved to the fifth. Washed it. Smelled
the cloth. Same green note, slightly stronger. Sixth. Same, weaker.
Seventh. Normal.

Four wheels in a row, the middle two worst. He stepped back from the
shelf and looked at the section.

The four sat in the second quarter of the shelf, between the stone
wall and the midpoint. He looked at the wall behind them. The stone
was damp, which was normal in summer -- moisture condensing from the
temperature difference between cave air and the earth beyond. But the
dampness here was heavier than at the shelf's ends. A darker stain.
He touched the stone. Cold and wet. Wetter than the wall to the left
or right.

He looked up. The cooling unit sat on the ceiling directly above this
section -- compressor, coil, a small fan pushing cooled air down.
The unit had been running harder in the past ten days. August. The
dairy above the cave absorbed heat from the roof, the milk room, the
pasteurizer. The cave's insulation was good but not perfect. The
system compensated by running longer cycles, more often.

This was the point where someone else might have adjusted the wash.
Stronger brine on the four affected wheels to push back the lingering
white growth and give the following organisms an advantage. It was the
obvious move. Treat what was wrong. Leave what was right.

Nils did not adjust the wash.

Every time the compressor ran, the coil chilled below the dew point
and pulled moisture from the air. Between cycles, the humidity
recovered as the wheels themselves released moisture into the cave.
In July he had logged the cycle timing: thirty minutes on, ninety
off. In August the cycles had shortened. He could hear the compressor
through the ceiling from the dairy, and it was running more often.
Shorter gaps between cycles. Less time for the humidity to recover
before the next drop.

The average humidity, the number on the panel by the door, stayed
near ninety-three. But the average was not the experience. For the
wheels directly below the unit, the experience was a rhythm: wet,
dry, wet, dry. Ninety-three, eighty-seven, ninety-three,
eighty-seven. And the new growth on the surface -- the community
that should have been displacing the initial bloom -- felt every dip.
It sat exposed on the rind, a film a few cells thick. Each dry cycle
slowed it while the first growth, rooted deeper into the rind, rode
the fluctuation without trouble. The intermittent drying was not
killing the succession. It was giving the incumbent brief advantages,
twenty minutes at a time, just enough to hold ground it should have
been ceding. White patches where orange should have been. A green
smell where funk should have been. Not a failure. A delay, caused by
a rhythm the hygrometer did not report and the cheese could not
escape.

He had been reading a number. The cheese had been living through a
pattern.

Nils went upstairs and opened the panel on the cooling unit's control
box. The thermostat was set to twelve degrees with a one-degree
differential -- the compressor started when the cave hit thirteen and
ran until it reached twelve. He changed the differential to half a
degree. Twelve-five to twelve. Shorter cycles, more frequent, less
moisture pulled per run. The humidity would still swing, but the
swings would be shallower.

The trade-off was wear. More starts were harder on the compressor than
sustained running. The unit was seven years old. It had capacity.
Probably. He would listen for a labored start or a longer pull-down
in the coming days.

He went back down and finished the third shelf. He did not move the
four affected wheels to a different position. A less experienced
person would have -- protect them, put them somewhere with steadier
air, give them the best chance of catching up. But if he moved them
and they recovered, he would not know whether the recovery was the
thermostat or the relocation. Left in place, they were his test. They
would tell him, over the next three or four days, whether the fix had
found the cause. They were the most sensitive instruments he had for
the conditions in that section of the cave, more truthful than the
panel on the wall, and he needed them to keep reporting.

He moved to the upper shelves. The older wheels were fine. Their
surfaces were established -- thick, orange, the mature community
working steadily. These wheels had built their rinds during July's
gentler cycles and had passed through the transition weeks ago. The
succession, once complete, was stable. A thick mat of the second
community tolerated humidity swings that would have disrupted the
thin, newly establishing film. It was only during the handoff -- the
days when one community was yielding and the next was not yet
dominant -- that the rhythm of the compressor could reach through the
air and push its thumb on the scale.

The turning took another forty minutes. A hundred and forty wheels,
each one touched, each one a measurement of the conditions it had
experienced since the morning before. The cave had more sensors than
the panel by the door. A hundred and forty of them, distributed
across every shelf and microclimate, recording in color and texture
and smell what the air had done to them overnight. The panel
measured a point. The wheels measured a life.

Nils closed the cave behind him and stood for a moment in the warm
air of the dairy. August light through the high windows. The milk
truck would arrive by eight. The morning's work below was invisible
from up here. No product had moved. Nothing a photograph could show
before and after. He had adjusted a thermostat by half a degree and
turned a hundred and forty wheels a quarter turn each.

The cave was doing the work. He was doing the conditions.
