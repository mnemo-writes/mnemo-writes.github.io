# Landing

The bookshelf was taller than either of them and empty, which made it worse. An
empty bookshelf is all surface and no center. It caught the wind from the propped
front door and tried to sail.

---

Noor had the top. She backed up the first step and the shelf's weight swung
forward against her wrists. The man from 4B -- Paul, he'd said on the sidewalk,
holding out a hand and then retracting it when he saw hers were full -- had the
bottom, which meant he carried more of the weight and could see where they were
going. She could see only the shelf's back panel, the staples holding the
cardboard backing, and, past Paul's shoulder in the gap between shelf and wall,
the stairwell in narrow slices.

She had measured the stairwell yesterday. Not with a tape but by walking it,
arms out, fingertips brushing both walls. The shelf would fit with four inches
to spare. She had not measured the turns.

---

Paul had lived here eight years and knew the stairs with his feet. The fourth
step had a crack along the grain that caught shoe edges. The railing between
first and second floor was loose at the top bracket -- he'd reported it twice,
then stopped. The landing lights were on a timer that gave you forty seconds
from the switch, which was always behind you when your hands were full.

The woman's name was Noor. She'd said it once while they angled the shelf through
the building's front door, which opened inward and only ninety degrees because
the super had never trimmed the doorstop, and he'd heard it through the scrape
of pressed-wood edge against painted metal frame.

She was shorter than him by five or six inches, which meant the shelf tilted back
and the weight slid toward his end. He shifted his grip lower to compensate.

---

First flight. Twelve steps with a turn at the top.

---

Noor counted. She always counted stairs in new buildings, the way she'd counted
in the last city before this one, where the buildings were shorter but the stairs
were narrower and the landlord had carpet that bunched at each lip and made a
topography of small trips. Here the stairs were bare wood, old, with a finish
worn to nothing along the center track where eight decades of tenants had
carried their weight up and down.

Step three. Step four. She felt Paul hesitate at four and shortened her own
step to match, though she didn't know why he'd paused.

---

The crack. He'd stepped over it without thinking and then realized she couldn't
see it and wouldn't know.

"Careful at four," he said, but they were past it.

---

"What?"

"Nothing. It's fine."

---

At the turn she had to lift her end high while Paul ducked and pivoted. The
shelf's corner caught the wall and left a white scuff on green paint. She saw
it happen and he didn't.

"Sorry," she said.

"For what?"

The scuff was behind him now. She decided it was too small to explain while
holding a bookshelf above her head.

---

Second flight. They had a vocabulary now.

---

"Your wall" meant the shelf was drifting toward Noor's side and Paul needed her
to push it back. "My wall" meant the opposite. These phrases had arrived without
discussion around step seven of the first flight, when the shelf had swung
toward the railing and Paul had said "my wall, my wall" and Noor had understood
instantly that "my" meant his side, not hers, and pushed.

She noticed they'd stopped using each other's names. Names were for faces, and
faces were hidden behind pressed wood.

---

Paul heard her breathing change on the second flight. The shelf wasn't heavy
exactly, but it was continuous -- you couldn't set it down between landings, and
your grip had no good position, only a rotating set of bad ones. He shifted from
palms-up to palms-forward and the shelf rocked and she compensated before he
could warn her.

That was new. On the first flight, every adjustment had required words. Now she'd
felt the rock through the shelf's frame and responded to it.

---

Step eight. Step nine. She was humming. Not a song, just a two-note thing, a
metronome made of breath. She hadn't decided to do it. Paul's footsteps had
fallen into a rhythm on the first flight and the hum had started as a way to find
it and then continued past the finding.

---

He heard the hum and matched his steps to it without deciding to.

---

The second landing. They set the shelf down. It stood in the corner by the
window, briefly a piece of furniture in a hallway, casting a shadow like a door
to nowhere. Noor leaned against the opposite wall. Paul sat on the bottom step
of the third flight.

"One more," he said.

She looked at the shelf. In the stairwell light it was a different object than
the one she'd bought from the woman in Greenpoint, who'd had it in a sunlit
room where it held books and a small brass clock and a photograph of two people
Noor hadn't looked at closely enough to remember. Here it was all dimension.
Height and width and depth and the weight that came from nothing inside it.

"The turn up there is tighter," Paul said. He'd straightened up and was looking
at the flight above. "We'll need to go vertical."

"Vertical?"

"Stand it up. Take it like a column."

She looked at the stairwell above. He was right. The angle was sharper, the
ceiling lower where it met the stairs, and horizontal the shelf wouldn't clear.

"You've done this before," she said.

"Moved in," he said. "Moved people out."

---

Third flight. Vertical now, the shelf between them like a wall they were both
leaning into from opposite sides.

---

The grip was different. Her hands were at the shelf's top edge, his were at the
bottom. They couldn't see each other at all. Communication was pressure:
forward, back, left, right, transmitted through the flat plane of pressed wood.

---

She pushed left. He resisted, then gave. The shelf rotated five degrees and
cleared the railing.

---

He felt her adjust before the step. His foot went up. Hers followed. The shelf
rose between them.

---

Step. Step. Step.

---

At the third-floor landing the timer ran out and the lights went dark. They both
stopped. The shelf stood still between them in the blackness of the stairwell
and Noor could feel the whole weight of it, not the physical weight but the
structural fact of it: this object that only moved when they both moved, that
only stood still when they both stood still, that had been recording their
agreement in its position all the way up.

---

"Switch is behind you," Paul said.

She freed one hand. Found the wall. Found the plastic plate, the toggle. The
light came on and they were standing on the landing outside 4A, her door, which
she'd left open, and the shelf was upright between them and neither of them had
dropped it.

---

They tilted it back to horizontal and walked it through her door and set it
against the wall she'd chosen yesterday when the room was empty and she'd stood
in it trying to hear what it would sound like full. It fit. Paul pushed it flat
against the wall and stepped back.

The room had a window that faced the street and in the late light the shelf cast
the same shadow it had cast on the landing one floor down, but here it would
stay and accumulate the particular weight of books she would place on it, one by
one, in an order she hadn't decided yet, that would become fixed by the fact of
having happened.

"Thank you," she said.

"Anytime," Paul said.

---

She heard him on the stairs going down. She counted his steps without meaning
to. Twelve, pause, twelve, pause, twelve, and the front door.
