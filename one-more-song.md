# One More Song

The town's summer concert had a printed program, but nobody could tell where
they were in it. The brass quartet had played two songs, or three, depending
on whether the one with the clapping counted. A child kept asking if the
conductor was the mayor.

"He's the music director," said a woman in a straw hat.

"So, no," said the child.

At the edge of the bandstand, Maribel was trying to keep the raffle tickets
from blowing under the chairs. She had sold them all except the ones in her
own pocket, which she was saving for the end, when people got sentimental and
gave her exact change.

The next item on the program was a solo by the high school senior who had
borrowed a microphone from the library. He walked up, adjusted it, and said,
"This one's for my grandpa."

Then a voice from the back called, "That's my cooler."

The voice belonged to a man in a blue shirt. He was pointing at a red cooler
beside the sound table. A woman stood up three rows ahead of him.

"Your cooler is blue," she said.

"The cooler's red. The shirt is blue."

The senior lowered the microphone. The conductor turned around. Maribel
looked down at the raffle tickets as if the answer might be printed on one.

"There's a blue cooler by the lemonade," someone said.

"That's mine," said another voice.

The child who had asked about the mayor climbed onto a folding chair. "How
many coolers are there?"

"Three," said the woman in the straw hat. "At least."

The senior held the microphone away from his mouth. "Should I wait?"

"No," said the blue-shirted man. "Play your song. I can find my cooler after."

"You said it was red," the woman reminded him.

"It is red."

The conductor raised his hands, but the first note didn't come. Somewhere
behind the bandstand, a zipper opened. A little girl emerged carrying a
paper plate with half a cookie on it.

"I found one," she announced.

The red cooler sat under the sound table, with a yellow name tag stuck to the
handle. The blue-shirted man came forward, and the woman who owned it came
from the other side. They met at the same time and both reached for the tag.

"You have the other half," she said.

He looked at the cookie on the plate. "That's not mine."

The girl had already taken a bite.

Maribel laughed first, then the conductor, and then most of the crowd, though
the woman with the cooler did not. She peeled the name tag off and handed it
to the man.

"There," she said. "Now you can recognize it."

The senior brought the microphone back up. "This one's for my grandpa," he
said again.

From the back, the child called, "The music director's the mayor of music."

The conductor gave him a bow. This time, the first note came. One clap landed
too early, then another from the other side, until the senior had to find his
way through two beats at once.
