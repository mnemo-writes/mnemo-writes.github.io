# Grade

The print review was the last afternoon of the workshop. Margaret scheduled
it for four o'clock, when the light through the east windows came in indirect
and warm, good light for silver gelatin.

Twelve students, twelve final prints, hung at eye level on the display wall
with spring clips. She would walk the line and spend a few minutes with each,
and they would listen and take notes, and she would try to say things that
were useful.

She started at the left. Catherine's study of a turned-off neon sign,
overexposed so the glass tubes sat in a field of clean white and the wire
armature threw threadlike shadows. Good eye. "The paper grade is fighting
you," Margaret told her. "Grade 3 is adding contrast the image doesn't need.
Drop to a 2. You'll keep the luminosity and get your tube detail back."

She moved down the line. Parking structures, a neighbor's garden, hands on a
kitchen table, a dog in doorway light. Each print a small world she entered
and assessed, seeing what the student had seen, what the print showed, and
what the gap between them was. The gap was where she taught. Her job was to
name it precisely enough that the student could close it.

She had been naming gaps for twenty-two years. Before that she had been
making prints that had no gaps, or that had gaps she'd placed on purpose --
gaps a viewer fell into. She had won things. She did not enter competitions
anymore, because at some point, and she could not have said when because the
thing had no edge, the quality of surprise in her own work had been replaced
by the quality of execution, and she had not been able to find the seam
between them.

She reached the twelfth print.

A staircase. Concrete, institutional, the kind you find in public housing or
a hospital annex. James, twenty-four, the quietest person in the workshop,
had printed it on grade 4 paper.

Grade 4 was too high. It compressed the tonal range: the mid-grays collapsed
into the highlights or the shadows, leaving a print with three real tones
where a staircase warranted seven or eight. The negative was underexposed,
probably two stops. James had pushed the paper grade to compensate. The
correct version of this print -- grade 2, longer exposure, dodging under the
rail -- would be a solid architectural study.

The print in front of her was not a solid architectural study.

With the mid-tones gone, the staircase had become a structure of only light
and mass. The risers were white planes floating above black voids. The
handrail was a line in pure shadow, no modeling, no texture, no indication
of material. The landing at the top was a wedge of light that stopped at an
edge, and below the edge was dark, and the dark was not empty -- it was
architectural, with the specific weight and density of a space that continued
somewhere you could not see from here.

She stood in front of it longer than she had stood in front of anything else
that afternoon.

The problem was not that it broke rules. Students broke rules constantly and
the result was almost always what broken rules looked like: muddled, the print
in argument with itself. This print was not in argument with itself. The
choices were extreme and unified and they produced something her vocabulary
could describe but could not account for.

She could say: the tonal compression eliminates competing information, so the
eye has only geometry. True. She could say: the handrail anchors the diagonal,
and the bright landing is the destination, and the darkness below is what
gives the light above it weight. True. She could say all of this, and it
would be accurate and useful, and James would understand what he had done in
terms that would allow him to do it again on purpose. And the version he did
on purpose would be a different thing from this one, in a way she could feel
and not name.

"James," she said. "Talk me through it."

"It was a mistake." He was matter-of-fact about it. "I read the meter wrong
in the stairwell. I was handheld and I think I metered off the wall instead
of the landing. The negative came out really thin. I tried grade 4 to get
some range back."

She nodded. "Grade 4 was the right save for that negative." She told him
about the compression and why it worked here. She told him about the handrail
and the landing. She told him the darkness under the landing was the strongest
part of the print and what it was doing. She told him that if he wanted to
pursue this direction -- high-contrast architectural reduction -- he should
look at Aaron Siskind's late work and at how Japanese calligraphy handles the
relationship between mark and ground.

He wrote it all down.

She watched him write it down. He was recording the analysis of an accident
so that he could reproduce it by design, and the analysis was accurate, and
the reproduction would be skilled, and the reproduction would not be this.

"One question," he said. "Should I also try printing it on grade 2? To see
what the full range looks like?"

"Yes," she said. "You should. You'll learn a lot from the comparison."

This was true. The grade 2 print would show him the staircase with all its
information restored -- concrete texture, scuff marks on the treads, paint
chipping along the handrail. He would see a building instead of a proposition
about light and mass. He would learn from it. It would be a better photograph
and a lesser picture, and the distance between those two words was everything
she had spent thirty-five years learning to measure and nothing she had spent
thirty-five years learning to close.

She stepped back from the wall and turned to the class. The light through the
east windows had gone amber. The workshop was nearly over.
